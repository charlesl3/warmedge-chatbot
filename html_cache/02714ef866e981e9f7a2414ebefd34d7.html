Welcome to figure skating!

Rate of progress really depends on a number of factors beyond natural ability, or the expertise of your coach.

Here are some of them: whether you had basic skating previously, how much relevant other training you have in dance in acrobatic sports [ie. ballet, gymnastics], how old you are [acrobatic sports are easier to learn before the adolescent growth spurt], what federation you are in [learning sequences vary], and how many sessions a week you are skating, and whether you are in group or private lessons.

There is a lot of information on the Lutz Corner section of GS, and your thread will be moved to that location very soon as it is the place dedicated to this kind of discussion.

Questions similar to yours come up frequently, so gkelly has been trying to get a basic information thread going that could be in the "permanent thread category" It's called "So you want to be a figure skater..." you may wish to take a look at that one here.

If your country's skating federation has a Long Term Athlete Development [LTAD] Model as Canada's does, it may provide you benchmarks and milestones in terms of amount of average training time to reach certain skills. For example, under Canada's model you would be at the end of the basic skating program 'CanSkate' completing CanSkate Stage 6, and starting into STARSkate in the "Learn to train" stage of the LTAD Model, with about 2 sessions a week to start working up to 4 sessions a week before moving on to the next stage ['learn to compete']. But as you mention the half-flip, you are not likely in Canada as that is not part of the required skills at this point..

One concern that I have about your post is that all the skills you mention as figure skating skills are what we refer to as basic agility skills, but development of balance and control are crucial at your level as well. In Canada, skaters can progress to work on more advanced stages in one area while they are still working on another [eg. faster on agility than on control], but there is an absolute requirement to keep a helmet on until Stage 5 is passed.

Knowing you are not in Canada, I'm providing the CanSkate report card just for reference so that you can see what I mean for Stage 6 [note there are some optional hockey skills for skaters on that track]. A waltz jump is listed as " Rotating power jump (fwd 180°)" in Stage 6, two foot spin is in Stage 4...

Each country's system and requirements are a bit different at present as new sports science knowledge is leading to significant system changes in the 'big' skating federations.

No, that progress is fine! First of all, pretty much all skaters will tell you they are still working on perfecting crossovers no matter what level! If you're in the US, you can look at taking the Pre-Preliminary Moves in the Field test, or if you're an adult, Adult Pre-Bronze moves in the field.

You may find that as you go on, some skills come really easily and some skills that are at the same 'level' are a lot more difficult for you. The great thing about figure skating is that you never run out of things to work on!

Going from skating 2 hrs a week to 8-10 hours the next week is a huge jump. Please be careful not to sustain any injury from being unaccustomed to extended periods on ice.

ETA: By injury, I don't mean only injury from falling. Injury also includes overuse injuries and foot injuries (blisters, bunions, bone bruises, muscle strains, tendonitis, stressed ligaments, the list goes on) due to extended periods of wearing ill-fitting skates. If you're still using rental skates, bear in mind that rental skates are not designed for foot comfort, so I would suggest getting your own pair sooner than later, and make sure they are fitted correctly.

I know sometimes it seems boring to not be always working on jumps or spins but in the long run I believe it will make you a stronger skater.

Seconding this. It honestly wasn't until I was skating several hours (6-10) a week and really focusing on edges and working my bronze dances that my edges REALLY are at the stage where they are not perfect but really darn good. And that was after years of skating.

There's so much to the edges alone from body alignment to proper foot placement to making sure all lobes are even to making sure your arms aren't helicoptering as you transition them that maybe for the little time that OP has been on the ice they're good for the little time they skated, but perfected? I'm guessing not.

@PinkIce - Don't get discouraged from reading others' posts. I've seen many posts in which the skater claims to be doing elements perfectly, only to finally see a video and find they are no where near that. Focus on your own skating and progress and not the progress of others. There's a man at my rink that on paper is working on novice moves, but visually he's barely past pre-pre in terms of skill. He figured out how to go fast and his control is incredibly lacking. But if you saw what he was working on on paper you'd say "Wow, he only started skating two years ago, how is he already at novice??". He didn't pass pre-juv (despite saying he did) so it's all talk. He can say all he wants but until the visual proof is there then it is just that, talk.
