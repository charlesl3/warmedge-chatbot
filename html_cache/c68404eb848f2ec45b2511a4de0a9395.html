In kid skaters, I see generally two types of skaters. The competitive skater and social skater. Both are committed skaters but which type depends on the family situation, meaning what does the skater want and how committed is the family.

Social skaters practice a couple of times a week, take tests, and participate in competitions. They will make progress but not at the same pace as competitive skaters. But those families keep time and financial commitments to a "minimum." Each family defines that minimum a different way.

Competitive skaters have a family life that revolves around skating. Lots of time and money is poured into skating. For those families, the question is how long is the family (and skater) willing to sustain that lifestyle? Results (due to lack of progress, injuries, puberty) will probably determine when the family decides to scale back. At least with us, if you had told me when my daughter was 5 that in 8 years my family would be so committed to ice skating, I would have laughed at you and told you you were crazy. What happens is that each year is a slow evolution. You think nothing more can be added but guess what, the coach decides to slowly add xx so there goes some more time and money.

Switching from a competitive skater can be a good thing. The kid still gets to do what they love (skating) but not at that high level.

fyi - I have always encouraged my daughter to have other activities in addition to skating. With our hectic ice skating schedule it is extremely hard but I think it is extremely important for her to have other interest beyond skating. So we find a way to fit it all in.

"so after skating for so many years, what do these kids do for an activity? Or do you mean quitting after they graduate high school and go to college? There is so much nonsense going on as is, maybe I need to try harder to get my daughter to like something else."

I started dancing (ballet, jazz and modern) lessons at 4 years, stayed on much longer in the recreational area than most, but quit at 17. The actual point I quit at was when I had started my driving lessons and between school and other commitments I just found too little time to book actual driving lessons. time had to be made, so dancing lost - as I was in adult dance classes by then, dominated by people who prepared for pro applications, and felt very ill-fitting, it was easy to let go at that time.

And for a young kid, parents set the frame of what they're interested in. later, as you might recall from your own teenage years, interests crop up themselves. a social life that requires more upkeep (small children don't require long talks to keep up with a friendship!), a lot more school assignments and homework, longer school hours, school clubs and activities, and newly-aquired interests just keep popping up in a teenager's life. Media interest is something that teenagers spend a lot of time on - being a fan of music, tv shows, interacting on social media or youtube. sometimes it's plain old reading they get interested in. the possibilities are endless.

People who worry their kids could have "too few" interests whould think back on how much they actually did in their teenage years. And yes, even if that crush on a tv star or musician was stupid and didn't last long: it's still an interests, and having those experiences is something that develops character when growing up.

If injury prevents a kid from continuing with what they love to do, alternative interests might have to be nurtured, but in most other cases: don't bother. your kid will always find something they like to spend time on. as long as it's not dangerous to their health, support them.
