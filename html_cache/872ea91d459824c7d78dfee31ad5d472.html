When I started as an adult, I jumped right into private lessons for the reasons you mentioned - the ability to have a bit more flexibility in terms of scheduling, and to get lessons at a time that worked for me (7:30pm on a Tuesday is apparently not a hot time for group classes. Go figure. :lol: )

Is there a group class near you that's just for adults? If so, then that could be fun. You'd be around other adult beginners, which is pretty cool. To me, that'd be the main advantage of the group class, versus saving money - something that really won't happen for you if you miss some of the classes.

My thought is that, if you can get to most of the group classes, and the class is for adults, sign up for the group class. See how you feel. If, at the end of the classes, you want to move into private lessons, do it.

i started with group lessons and i wish i had started with privates, i think i would have advanced a lot quicker.

the problem with group lessons (at least at my rink) is that the classes started getting significantly larger. the structure of the class wasn't like how you see toddlers have a class where everyone practices the same skill with structured exercises and drills. instead, every adult is off on their own and one of 3-4 coaches approaches them and works on whatever they happen to need help on. they stay with them for a few minutes (theoretically) and then move on to the next skater. and maybe you'll get another coach who drops by to help you out before your lesson ends.

when my classes started getting larger, i started getting less individual time with coaches. i remember one session, none of the coaches approached me until the very last minute of the lesson (which at that point just felt like a way to avoid any lawsuits or mean yelp reviews about how i paid for a lesson i never actually received). during that time that's how most of my lessons went, with just 1 or 2 minutes of instruction at the very end of class and that didn't help me improve at all. i was having trouble with one of the basic moves we had to learn and i honestly probably spent about 4 months working on that move with little to no progress because i never received the proper attention (and believe me i practiced a lot, so it was by no lack of effort. was i doing something wrong? i had no way of knowing). perhaps a private instructor could have individualized their attention more, and actually have the time to analyze what i was doing in order to best help me progress. perhaps they could have come up with drills that worked for me because they would have the proper time to get to see me attempt the skill, versus just having a rushed minute at the end of a group session where the coach barely has enough time to simply see you attempt the skill.

so i would suggest to start off with private lessons.

and that scheduling thing is correct, too. with group lessons i was assigned to a certain day of the week and time and if i missed it i could make it up on the other adult session but since i wasn't really progressing much week-to-week, it felt like a bit of a waste to have to pay for lessons that often when the spur of 1-2 minute instruction from coaches wasn't really enough to help me improve in that time frame. (for example i probably could have foregone lessons for 2 months and still be at the same level of progress.)

i made the calculations (for my rink, it might be different at yours) and it would have been a lot cheaper to simply take a private lesson every other week while paying for an annual public session pass instead of having to pay for a package of group lessons for a year.


there was a woman at my rink whom i became friends with and she started off with private lessons around the same time as me (i think she started a few months after me) and she really improved a lot and actually got to work on moves in the fields patterns and stuff like that (of course, you should never compare your progress with others because aptitudes are different as well) but she would occasionally give me tips that her coach had given her and it's little stuff like that that make you realize the quality of instruction is of a different level when you have a private lesson. (and this is with the same coach who teaches group lessons. simply having time to know your student and their way of learning and actually seeing their technique does wonders).



one thing i will say about group lessons though, i got to meet some people who were my age and that was pretty fun to then practice with them during public sessions. it adds a bit to the camaraderie and you get a little competitive with trying to learn stuff if you're at the same level. (but on the other hand, you might get disappointed and frustrated if you see them advance quicker than you if you get stuck on something for too long ) but it's a nice way to make friends. this didn't always happen though, for the first few months i didnt befriend anyone and people seemed a bit less friendly. so it's not a guarantee you'll make friends but it is a nice benefit of group lessons if you do end up making some.

The group lessons at my rink follow US Figure Skating's Learn to Skate program, with Adult levels 1 - 6.
Unlike yuzushenko's class, ours are led by an instructor who teach the skills to the group as a whole. I do find yuzushenko's class description interesting and sometime wish I could deviate and teach what the skater wants as I don't always agree with the skills demanded in each level (example: teaching a snowplow stop first).

On occasion if there is low enrollment a class will be a blend of 2 levels. In that instance skaters will be left to do some practicing on their own - usually the lower level so the higher level can be instructed on their skills. If the level 1's are comfortable enough I invite them to work on level 2 skills.

I'd say if you're just starting out and the group lesson time works for you then go that route. At my rink the enrollee gets the added bonus of the skating the public session that is before or after the group lessons for free. But you can't go wrong with private lessons if you can afford them.

And on rare occasion of a class only having 1 enrollee, they essentially got private lessons for 8 weeks!

I taught swim lesson for 20+ years and always observed that the students that made to best progress took a combination of group and private lessons. The private lessons gave the student technique and the group gave the student comradarie /peer pressure.

Years ago I took group lessons as an adult. While there were two adult classes advertised as beta and gamma, they were really a group of beginners and a group of much more advanced students. Unfortunately I seemed to fall in the middle of the two classes. But what I really liked about the classes was this instant group of skating friends rhat seemed to be at the rink regardless of when I went to practice. To me, that was the true benefit of the group classes.

At my rink, each class meeting inclues one public pass with rentals. So if your sessions has 8 classes, you also get 8 passes for public session valid while the class os going.

My rink also has a unusual feature. When you sign up for a session and know you will be miss a few classes, you can say you miss dates a, b, and c and only pay for the dates you plan to attend.

Honestly, as long as it makes financial sense, I have to suggest going right to private lessons.

Does your rink have general sessions that are just for adults, or at times most of the skaters are the regular attending adults? If so, those would get you the social and peer interaction you would get from group lessons, plus the adults working on higher level skills would be inspiring.

Since the rink here has adult sessions several times a week, and there are sessions that are usually just adults simply because of the scheduling, the adult skaters here have a tighter community than probably most rinks. The adults usually take their private lessons during those sessions. Also, sometimes adult skaters who already are taking private lessons will get together and arrange for "custom classes" with preferred coaches to work on specific skills together, and those are treated like any other group classes as far as cost and practice cards (they still do private lessons).

I'll weigh in on the side of privates; this assumes that your priority is learning to skate, rather than socializing. The only downside to privates is the cost. But this is an instance in which you get what you pay for; assuming a proper coach, of course.

One of the key roles of a coach is to analyze what you are doing wrong and develop a remedial course of action. In general, you will get this individualized attention with private lessons, but not with group lessons (unless you luck out with those outlier scenarios in which you are in a group class of one). Also, with privates, the coach will tailor the program to your mode of learning, rate of progress, strengths, weaknesses, personality, likes, dislikes, and goals. But group lessons generally march along to a preset program.

Another thing to consider. Private lessons are generally pay as you go. If you don't find your coach satisfactory, you can drop her and find another one (this assumes, of course, that more than one coach is available in your area at suitable times). And you continue consistently with a single coach: if either you or your coach can't make a particular lesson for whatever reason (illness, travel, business, ...), you simply reschedule. With group lessons, you generally prepay in advance for all the lessons. If you're not happy, generally no refunds. You are not guaranteed the same coach (which some see as a plus, but generally is a minus). And there are either no makeups, or limited makeups (again, with some outlier exceptions).
