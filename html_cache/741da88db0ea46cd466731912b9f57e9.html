I think it kinda sucks that people have to hold back on taking the FS tests in order to remain in the level they can reasonably compete in.
For example, you want to take the FS tests to "verify" to the world that you have certain skills, but you don't want to "move up" in competition levels because you know the other girls are far more advanced in competition than what is required on the FS test.
I almost feel like there should be a checkbox on the FS test that says "audit only" or "I want my skills verified to the world, but I don't want to move up in competition levels."
Do they really need to force people to compete at higher levels just because they took a FS test? I know they do this to avoid people competing in lower levels and "sandbagging" the competition. But it feels there could be a better way to defend against this issue than using FS tests. They already list which jumps etc are or are not allowed in a certain level. They already allow us to test all the way up to Senior MIF test without changing our competition level. So why not allow us to complete FS tests without changing our competition level (as long as you list what jumps/spins/skills are allowed in the comp category)? Is it the "lure" of completing more FS tests that they think will keep people from sandbagging competitions?

I'm a "completionist", so its my dream goal to get thru ALL FS tests one day (I hope). But I don't expect to be "good" or "great" at those tests (since I'm such a late starter). I just want to be "good enough" to pass the test, and at the same time, still compete at a lower level that isn't insanely difficult for me.

Even if you aren't a late starter, some people just don't have the natural talent to compete at a higher level, and therefore they never complete their FS tests (even tho they might really want to) since they have to stay at the competition level where they can survive and not be utterly destroyed.


Thoughts? ideas? comments?

I'd suggest competing one level higher than your test level at a local competition, since most of them allow it. In the near future, the testing structure will probably be opened up to allow skaters to request that their competition performance be used as their freestyle test. This proposal is already being discussed at USFSA.

USFSA has a "test track" option in most club competitions. The elements in the test track programs are restricted to the required elements in the free skate test (for example, in test track novice level competition only double loop, salchow, and toe loop are allowed, no double flip or lutz, double axel, or triple jumps). Is this sort of what you're describing?

It doesn't work out perfectly, this is true. If you have a double axel and want to compete with it, for example, test track is probably not for you, but you also would not likely be competitive at the standard senior level without any triple jumps.

Re: Adults taking the senior free skate test, I am 26 and planning to take mine this year (although I've had to take some time off from skating recently so it'll be a while before I get back into practicing the program). I started skating as a child but I was not a highly competitive skater. I don't feel like I've lost any of my ability to skate and do programs and jumps just because I got older - health, fitness level, time, and money are more prohibitive than age itself I would think. Some of these things may correlate with age to a degree, but certainly there are adults in their 20's, 30's, and beyond out there who are still working on the standard tests.

In my perfect world, there would be no tests at all. You can try out for the NFL even if you've never played football before! There are so many reasons why the test system is counterproductive, especially in areas of the country where test sessions are difficult to find and difficult to enter. Ice time is generally at a premium in these areas, too, which leaves whole areas of the country struggling to advance -- half your time needs to be devoted to learning and mastering moves patterns. It's unnecessarily prohibitive and I think it also makes the sport very confusing.

Skating skills come with more time on the ice. If you've mastered the jumps and spins you think you need to be competitive at say, novice, then you've likely spent enough time on the ice developing your skating skills enough that you'd be able to pass a novice moves test. Realistically, if you're competing at novice well balanced, you're likely much more proficient at moves than what's on the novice test since most people test ahead anyway. If not, well... you probably won't do that well in competition.

I know tests are generally profitable fundraisers, but I think they should go.

That is probably not true. The novice MIF test, for example, is the hardest one to pass because it includes the most new skills as well as expecting a higher quality of execution than intermediate.

A skater who spends all her time focusing on freestyle skills like jumps and spins might not bother learning twizzles and loops and rockers and choctaws at all if they don't interest her.

A competitive skater who wants to get higher IJS levels on the step sequences and more credit for Skating Skills and Transitions would probably make the effort to learn enough of those skills to earn those points, but she probably would stick to the directions that are most comfortable for her and not learn everything on both feet, inside and outside edges, forward and backward. Without those skills, she would not be able to pass the tests.

(The same is true of brackets and forward twizzles, and even maybe backward threes and outside mohawks, at intermediate and juvenile levels. If you don't need them to pass freestyle tests or to compete at all in freestyle, many skaters would not bother learning them. And then their lower skill level would show up in their programs and they would earn lower program component scores than other skaters with the same or easier jumps and perhaps not understand why.)

If not, well... you probably won't do that well in competition.
