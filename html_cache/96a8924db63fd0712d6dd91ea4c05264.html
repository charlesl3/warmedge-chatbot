My honest answer is there's no such thing. If you have any intention of doing even low level jumps and spins, you're better off starting with a competitive level skate, which means buying boots and blades separately, which means $$$. But truthfully, I believe you will spend less in the long run if you know you want to continue skating. If you get a beginner level skate, you will have to replace it in no more than 6 months to a year max. And even the beginner skates are not exactly cheap. So you might as well put the money you would spend on that skate towards a higher level skate that will last you a long time. As an adult, you know your feet won't grow any more, so the only reason you would need to replace skates is if you break them down, which will take much longer with a better boot. (You might need to replace blades eventually if you skate a LOT but that's only if you skate a TON).

But regardless of what level skate you decide to go with MAKE SURE you are fitted by someone who knows what they are talking about, and don't allow yourself to be rushed through the process. There is absolutely nothing more important about buying skates than making quadruple sure you have a good fit. Because I have just recently been through this process, so learn from my example.

I am in my early 30s and returning to skating for the first time since high school. I started lessons in January. I had very good skates when I was a teenager, and they're actually still in good shape, but I grew out of them. So I knew I would need new skates. Rental skates aren't good for anything but twisting an ankle tbh. So I went to get fitted at my rink's pro shop and ended up getting the Jackson Elle. This is actually a halfway decent skate that comes with blades, but the person who fitted me really kind of rushed me through trying on a few different sizes of Jacksons and Riedells (which are the only two brands they carry). It had been a LONG time since I'd been on the ice, and I didn't really remember the details of how a good skate should fit. (Take this advice for free: if your heel lifts up or your foot moves around AT ALL it is TOO BIG DO NOT BUY IT). Long story short I am now stuck with a pair of skates that is too big that I paid close to $300 for and if I want to continue skating safely, I now need to buy a better pair fitted by someone with more experience, and I'm not made of money, so this is terrible news. Don't be like me, kids. It's not fun.

Now that I've got that out of the way, here's the gist of what I've found in my recent research. Riedells are good if you have a pretty normal, medium width foot. Jacksons may be a better fit if you have a wider foot. Risports are good for a narrower foot. There are a few other brands out there like GAM, SP-Teri, and Edea, but I don't have any firsthand experience with any of those, so I can't really speak to them. Then there's also John Wilson and a couple of other companies that only make blades that you could look into if you choose to go the separate boots and blades route. (I highly, highly recommend the Wilson's Coronation Ace blade).

Ultimately, you have to decide how much you're willing to spend and what level of skating you are aiming for. There are a lot of sites out there that compare the various skates and boots. And all of the brand websites will tell you what the boot/blade/skate is "graded" for. That means what level of skills you can expect to do with it safely. Unless you only want to skate forwards in a circle DO NOT get a "recreational" level skate. No matter how good those prices look. You also don't want to go TOO stiff. i.e. probably don't get a boot graded for double jumps, because it will be way too stiff and you'll have trouble breaking it in.

Honestly, despite the fact that it doesn't fit me, I think the Jackson Elle is actually a decent starter skate. But I'm so serious about the fit issue. Ask around to a few people if you know anyone else who skates in your area and get some recommendations. Your rink's pro shop may be good, but it's better to get some outside feedback before making your choice. If you don't know anyone in your area ... maybe go out to a public skate and look for the people who know what they're doing and ask where they bought their skates. Awkward, but better than nothing.

Anyway, sorry for this novel! I hope it helps though.

In addition to Jackson & Reidell, Graf also do decent skates for people starting out - my first pair were Graf 500s, cost less than £100 and got me through to single jumps. Edea Overtures are also not too expensive & still stiff enough to do single jumps. However, you might find yourself limited by whatever your skate dealer stocks, so don't get too attached to buying a particular brand. The most important thing is how they fit your foot - do not be tempted by getting something cheap online, you need a proper fitter because it's very different to how street shoes fit!

You could also try 2nd hand skates; as long as they aren't visibly creased, 2nd hand can be a good way of saving money before you decide if you want to commit to spending more money on this sport. But you have to be really careful, because if the skates are too broken down you won't get any support & there'll be an increased risk of injury; so again, only buy from a reputable shop, ideally that's recommended by your coach/more experienced skaters at the rink.

I disagree that you need competitive skates to start. But you don't want skates from a big box store. Bottom of the line Jackson / Riedell skates are also probably not a good investment. You probably should look at something that will carry you through beginning freestyle.

If Jacksons suit your foot, the Elle is a really nice skate for a serious beginner. It's heat moldable and has a cork / leather sole so blades can be adjusted if necessary (not true of most plastic soles). It comes as a set with the Ultima Mirage blade. It works well for serious beginners and should cost you around $250.

You can do some research here:

https://www.kinziescloset.com/skate-comparison-guide.html

But don't buy online.

The most important thing is good fit. Your shoe size will be different than your skate size. A good pro shop can fit you properly, suggest the right brand, and deal with any problems that arise.

I am speaking from experience working in an ice rink. The skates I think are best for a beginner who is presently just wanting to have fun without feeling like skating is a chore, are either Jackson Soft Skates or Reidell Soft Tek Skates. We even have a couple coaches who use them when teaching. They will be suitable up to the beginning freestyle level. They are more supportive and more comfortable generally than similarly priced standard skates, have a short break-in period, and are sturdy. If I was not planning to do freestyle again, I would put away my expensive figure skates and use Jackson Soft Skates.

If you're plan is to get into freestyle but you are a beginner, it is going to be a while before you are advanced beyond the usefulness of the skates I mentioned. Buying freestyle boots right off the bat might mean sacrificing part of your fun just for the sake of getting a skates that you aren't really ready to use. Standard freestyle skates are stiffer, have bigger toe picks, and take longer to break in. Once you are in love with skating to the point that learning higher skills is more important than comfort and ease, you won't mind the inconvenience of breaking in a good pair of mid-level freestyle skates.

I also agree with Tavi about not buying competitive boots to start with; don't overboot yourself at this point. You won't be strong enough to break-in stiff competitive boots. Plus, they will be really expensive and “what-if” skating doesn't work out for you.

Oh, don’t be shy. No breach of etiquette. Just let the instructor know that you are planning to sign up for the next session and want to be properly equipped in advance. It’s in the instructor’s self-interest to help you succeed. Here are my tips:


(1) In your price range, if you are buying new gear, you will be purchasing a pre-packaged kit (boot with a pre-mounted blade). Buy only a kit in which the blade is mounted to the boot with screws. That way, the blade mount can be readily readjusted, or the blade can be readily replaced. In rock bottom kits, the blades are mounted with rivets. Avoid those.


(2) Definitely buy from a pro shop with a fitter experienced in figure skates. Ask around. Don’t just default to the pro shop at your rink (if there is one). In particular, ask around for which pro shop does decent sharpening for figure skates. Even though you will be buying beginner skates, a decent sharpening is still important. If you buy skates from a pro shop, typically the first sharpening is thrown in as part of the deal; new out-of-the-box blades typically do need sharpening. One bad sharpening can ruin a pair of blades irrevocably, so buy from a pro shop that does good fittings and good sharpenings. The bread-and-butter at most pro shops is hockey. Often there may only be one guy who's good at fitting and sharpening figure skates. So, when asking for recommendations, in addition to the name of the right shop, get the name of the right guy.


(3) Instructors, skate techs, and individual skaters all have their personal biases with respect to brands and models. So sample as many opinions as you can. Ultimately, what’s important is what fits your feet best. That said, in your price range in the US, you should be able to find a suitable kit in the Riedell or Jackson lines (assuming no major abnormalities in your feet).
