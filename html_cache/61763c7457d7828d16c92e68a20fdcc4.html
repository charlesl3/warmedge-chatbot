Hello David!!
Welcome to the world of ice skating!!
I take it you're interested on figure skating more than freestyle or hockey, right? Then you'll find out that on your own it's terribly difficult to actually learn

It will save you time (and time is money!) to sign up for a learn-to-skate program. Once you have a solid basis it's easier to practice on your own. But this sport is super precise technically speaking, specially if it's figure skating you're interested in.
You could also hire a coach just for classes once a month or for little time each time, if money is a problem.
Trust me, when you try real coaching you won't want to go back to learning on your own!
You'll also have less risk of injuries that way!!

If you definitely can't learn on real classes then please be very careful. It is super easy to learn moves the wrong way, and then have extra effort to relearn them the right way... Specially things like crossovers, that are the base for everything. But if they're truly truly not an option then before trying a new move search for videos in the internet and learn the correct technique, then try, record yourself and compare. This is also a good complement when you're learning in classes.
You can also ask people around the rink (keep an eye out for those who actually have the good technique and are not just fooling around). Don't expect free classes, but they will be very friendly and helpful! If you keep an schedule you'll end up making friends.

Don't worry about those 10 year old, they have been skating for way longer than you have and are fearless! Be safe and be fun —and please please take classes even if it's just once in a while! You'll really feel the difference!

Happy skating!

First advice: Don't think of figure skating as being about "tricks" -- it's about controlling the movement of the blades on edges. The jumps, and to a lesser degree spins, that you see all rely on having good control of those blades.

The flashier moves have their basis in techniques that have been developed honed over a century and a half trial and error and codifying the knowledge of what works passed down from coaches to students. Do yourself a favor and take advantage of that body of knowledge.

A lot of the technique is about internal muscle actions that are not obvious from the outside, so trying to learn tricks just by watching can learn to really bad technique and bad falls and inability to avoid crashing into other skaters. Don't go that route.
You can't see the centrifugal and centripetal forces that skaters need to control with their bodies just by watching someone else execute the skills, but you can sure feel them in your body when you try.

If at all possible, take some lessons with a coach, someone who knows how the skills build on each other and can direct you toward what to practice. Group lessons will be more cost-effective; private lessons would be more efficient in improving your individual skills based on your current strengths and weaknesses and being tailored to your interests in what you want to achieve on the ice.

After you have developed some basic technique and technical understanding, you would be better positioned to start playing around on your own with what you can already do.

If in-person lessons are absolutely impossible for some reason, then make sure to learn the basics first. Check out youtube videos by knowledgeable instructors for pointers on the basic skills that lead from beginning skating to beginning figure skating:
One-foot glides. Stroking from one foot to the other pushing from the side of your blade not the toepicks. Crossovers forward and backward both counterclockwise and clockwise. Edges (half-circles on one foot at a time -- back inside edges are tricky for beginners, but you'll need solid back outside edges before you can land any jumps). Forward outside and inside three turns and forward inside mohawks (and stepping from a back outside edge to forward outside on the other foot, which is really a kind of mohawk and is pretty much required for learning waltz jumps and some other jumps).

After you have the basics of those skills, you'd be ready to start trying beginning freeskating moves like waltz-jumps and one-foot spins.

Along the way, you could try some two-foot spins or lunges or pivots, or jumping up and down in place on two feet. When you have a strong one-foot glide, you could try spirals or shoot-the-duck.

Think of drawing a 3 on the ice on one foot as an important trick. Drawing an 8 (one whole circle on each foot) would also be a useful skill to aspire to.

Find experts, online or in print if you can't take lessons, who will break down for you what you should be doing with your arms free leg and back and butt and abdominal muscles for each skill. If it's impossible to hire a coach to tell you what you're doing wrong and how to fix it, try videoing yourself.

If it is in figure skates and involves doing specific prescribed and recognizable moves, skills, elements, and patterns, then it is figure skating. Freestyle is one area of figure skating, identified by the inclusion of jumps and spins. Freestyle, dance, "school figures", and moves in the field are all in the category of figure skating. The term "figure skating" comes from the practice of doing traditional school figures which were required for all testing and competitions; school figures are no longer a requirement, but in some places, moves in the field tests have to be passed at each level before the corresponding freestyle test for each level (for example, in the USA, silver moves has to be passed before silver freestyle test can be taken). Recreational skating in figure skates is not necessarily figure skating.

What treesprite said is completely true. But I was thinking more of "freestyle skating", and not "freestyle figure skating".
Freestyle skating is usually done by people in hockey skates and it's just about doing flashy moves (the typical "crowd-pleasers" such as hydroblading, jumping and landing on both blades, hockey stops, etc) and having fun. Some (in my opinion the best of them) do have some formal training, but others are autodidact. Just search for it in YouTube to see what it looks like
Figure skating (both freestyle and otherwise) requires total coordination between your arms, shoulders, legs, head, absolutely everything. Other styles of ice skating are more forgiving and thus "easier" to learn on your own.

But if you're considering going to classes then absolutely do it (think of it as a Christmas present to yourself!!), and no worries-- you're welcome! Good luck
