I take it you are in the US? Are you in an area with plenty of clubs and nonqual competitions in driving distance, or would you probably need to travel?

What is your current skill level -- what can you do? Did you pass any tests when you were taking classes? Are you female?

Keep in mind that competitions are divided first by test level, and then if there are enough entries at a given level they are usually divided by age. If you enter a large competition, there might be several groups of girls at your level and you would probably be placed in a group with other teenagers.

(If you're male, it gets more complicated to find someone of a similar skill level to compete against, let alone whether they're close to your age. Look for large competitions that get more entries.)

Download the "Compete USA" document and look at the breakdown of levels on p. 6:
http://www.usfigureskating.org/programs?id=89713&menu=programs

The pages that follow detail the skills that are required/allowed at each level.

If you are taking group lessons or just working on beginning skating skills, no freestyle yet, you would probably want to compete at a "Compete USA" competition (called "Basic Skills" up to last year). Sometimes these are stand-alone competitions just for lower levels, and sometimes they're combined with a regular nonqual club competition.

If you're at the beginning levels of freestyle already but have not yet passed the pre-preliminary freestyle test, you would have several options.

*Free Skate 1-6 events as part of Compete USA

*Beginner and High Beginner introductory levels, which allow skills comparable to the Pre-Free Skate/Free Skate 1 and Freeskate 2/3 group classes.

*Test Track, starting with Pre-Preliminary (you can skate up to compete at the level of the test you're working on, or at the level of the most recent test you've passed -- these events have strict restrictions on program content that don't allow any skills much higher than what's required on the freestyle test)

*Well-Balanced free skate, starting with No-Test (again, you can compete at your current test level or skate one level up to the next level -- the competitions allow elements that aren't required on tests until several levels higher; e.g., well-balanced Preliminary allows some double jumps and well-balanced Pre-Juvenile allows more)

Once you've passed the Pre-Preliminary freestyle test, you can compete either Pre-Preliminary or Preliminary, either test track or well-balanced. When you've passed the Preliminary test, you can compete Preliminary or Pre-Juvenile.

Test Track is popular in some areas of the country where more of the clubs are fairly recreational or there just isn't a lot of figure skating ice time available. Areas with plenty of ice and a number of serious competitive skaters tend to focus more on Well-Balanced competition even at the lower levels. But older skaters are more likely to end up in Test Track than younger ones.


Before you can take any of the freestyle tests, you need to pass the Moves in the Field test at that level. Many skaters work one or more levels ahead in Moves but don't test up in freestyle until they're comfortable with most of the jumps and spins allowed (not necessarily required) at that level.

If you think you will be starting with almost no skills, taking group lessons, only skating a couple hours a week, you should probably plan to start competing with the Basic 1-6 and then Free Skate 1-6 levels.

If/when you can already do crossovers and forward and backward edges and forward three turns and beginning jumps and spins, and if you're interested in passing standard track tests, and if you're going to take private lessons and practice 3 or more days a week, then you might prefer to look at the Test Track or Well-Balanced competition tracks. Your coach would advise you about when you would be ready to test and to enter competitions.

If you want to learn skills more quickly and pass tests, usually it's better to start out focusing on practicing for tests rather than entering competitions. But if you can put in enough time, you can do some of each.

How quickly you progress will depend a lot on how much time you can put in on the ice and also the quality and amount of instruction you get. It's theoretically possible you could reach Preliminary or Pre-Juvenile level in 2 years or so, but not if you don't skate more than 2 hours a week.

Again, if you're concerned about competing against other skaters close to your age, try to find larger competitions to enter that tend to draw a lot of entries at your level, even if it means you have to travel.

Try searching online for results from past years of competitions you might be interested in. If you find one with, for example, No Test Girls Groups A, B, C, D, and E, the 16-year-olds are probably in group E. But different referees might divide the groups differently.

Welcome ennuijpg. I am only going to speak for a popular non-qualifying showcase competition held by my club. If you're not familiar with a showcase competition you might want to check them out. You have much freedom in music and interpretation of that music, and it's more fun than traditional competitions. You skate under a spotlight and there are usually categories for duets and groups.

I just looked at last year's entry levels. We had No Test categories with no age restrictions. As the levels moved up, there became age and testing parameters within the grid.
In the low-level categories we had a wide range of ages competing in those categories - little kids to adults.

If you are just skating for fun, you can also find out if there are any ISI rinks/coaches/competitions in your area. At lower freestyle levels, you still only have to test with your own coach, so there isn't money to shell out and judges to worry about. By the time you get done with college, you will be old enough to do USFSA adult track, which is a little more forgiving of late starters than standard track.

In my area the only teen event is Open Juvenile and it is Non Qualifying.

For all other competitions, you just sign up for your level and depending on the number of contestants, they may break the groups down into age ranges (I think ISI does this the best because if you are 18 and the next oldest is 13, they will give you your own category - USFS tends to just put you in the 13 year old's group.). Even beginning skaters can compete from basic skills. You should seek a coach who can help you plan skating goals. If you have no skating experience at all, You might try skating 2 days a week - one with a coach or class and one on your own. As you build comfort and skills, you can work your way to more days.classes/lessons.

The difference is that ISI allows a single-entry event in which the skater competes "against the book."

USFSA doesn't allow that. If you enter a 6.0 event and there's no one to compete against, the event may just be canceled, or you may be allowed to skate an "exhibition." If they call it a competition, you'll just get all first place ordinals and a gold medal no matter how badly you skate.

At IJS levels, a single entrant at a level can at least get scores. But IJS isn't used at beginner levels.

That is incorrect - Only Juvenile (14 and under) and Intermediates (18 and under) have age limits. The rest have none. My guess is the lower levels have none since they don't "feed" into a qualifying structure.

Also, if you compete following the "test track" route, there are no age restrictions.

NOTE: I looked at a competition announcement from this past summer and the only age restrictions were for Juveniles and Intermediates. Here is how it reads:

ELIGIBILITY/TEST LEVEL:
Test Level: Competition level is the highest test passed as of the entry deadline in the discipline the skater is entering. Entrants may skate one level above that for which they qualify, but they may not skate down in any event. Skaters may only enter one level for each type of event. Skaters who placed in the top four in a final round of their last qualifying competition in their divisions must move up one level, except for novice and higher.
Age Requirements: Skaters entering juvenile free skating events (IJS) must be under 14 years of age at the close of entries. Skaters entering open juvenile free skating events (IJS), must be at least 14 years of age at the close of entries.
Skaters entering beginner -- pre-juvenile events will be divided as closely as possible by age should the number of entries warrant more than one group.
