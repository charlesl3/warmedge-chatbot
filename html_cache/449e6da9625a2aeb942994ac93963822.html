Good start! A couple tips for you - I don't know anyone who can do a good axel that can't do more than 5-6 revs in backspin. You want to get that a lot stronger - like 10 revs at least, and your axel will come. Similarly, when you try your axel, just try landing in a backspin instead of trying to check out, which you're doing too soon. That'll trick your body into getting into a good air position. Basically I would not worry about the landing and checking out part until you are able to do the full rotation. You can practice landing and checking out on the backspin checkout drill or other single jumps.

If you can do off-ice axels, then it may be helpful to post them as well so that we can compare the differences.

For me, the biggest thing I notice is that you don't really get into a backspin position and/or you open up too early, however you want to put it. You free leg (left leg) goes out in front of you rather than sort of wrapping around your right leg (i.e. backspin position). Also, your left hip stays down once you're in the air rather than up (meaning you're not transferring your weight over to a backspin, as you say). When you're coming down for the landing, your arms are coming out too early.

As for how to fix it, it depends on what your coach has told you and how you're thinking about the jump. A common way to teach the axel is to think of it as if you're stepping up, i.e. you jump off your left foot, but as soon as you take off, once you're in the air, you push down hard with your right foot as if you're trying to go up some stairs. Also, as already said, you should try to stay in the backspin position instead of checking out too early. You can try doing waltz jumps into loop jumps and/or waltz jumps into backspins to get a feel for that, if you haven't already.

There are 2 things I see contributing to the problem:

(1) You are pulling your arms in too early. You should be fully backward over the right leg *with the left thigh lifted* before pulling the arms and free leg in. From what I'm seeing in your video, by the time you've gotten backward you are already pulled in, and that just kills the rotation (exactly like a backspin where you pull in without being open first). The timing is UP, HIP, ARMS.

(2) You aren't bringing the right leg through enough on takeoff. You bring the right knee through, but not the shin or the foot. That technique might work for advanced skaters doing double and triple axels, but I don't see anyone successfully using it on a single axel. Instead, think of leading with your right shin as you take off. Not the right knee (which is what you do now) and not the right foot. Lead with the right shin. Waltz-loop-loop is a good exercise to get yourself used to doing this and getting backward over the right side.

Oh I just mean that you're not shifting your weight over to your right side. Once you're in the air, if your weight's on your right side, then your left hip would be even or slightly raised, i.e. above your right hip. You don't necessarily need to actively think about raising the left hip though (which may throw your torso out of alignment), just that if you're thinking of the backspin position and getting your weight shifted, your left hip is naturally raised a bit. Instead, your left hip is lower than your right hip. So it means when you're in the air, you're not really thinking about putting your weight on your right side. That's kind of what the bench exercise does for you -- get you used to putting your weight onto your right side quickly.

From your off-ice axel (and on-ice ones), it looks like your torso is also a bit hunched in the air. Don't know if it's because you're not tightening your core enough. It's also evident that you're keeping a much tighter position off-ice than on the ice (in terms of where the free leg is) -- the skates do add quite a bit of weight swinging around when you're spinning in the air, so you have to work extra hard at keeping the free skate in when you're on the ice.

The axel is a jump to be patient about though. In some ways it's really your first double jump -- it shares a lot in common with the double salchow and the double toe.

Oh yeah I'll mention this briefly too. The on-ice axels had about 0.35 seconds of air time, or about 6 inches of height. The off-ice axels had about 0.42 seconds of air time, or about 8.5 inches of height. Basically your off-ice axel is about 40% higher than your on-ice axel. Now, it's typical to lose around say 10% of height when you take a jump onto the ice (because the skates weigh you down, and you're obviously not wearing them off-ice), but you shouldn't be losing that much if you were attacking them just as hard on the ice compared to your off-ice axel. So basically, yes, you could be jumping a lot harder on the ice. It's normal to be a bit scared though and hold back a bit, and the confidence to go for it more will come with practice. But the more you go for it, the more likely you'll be successful.

I missed the video of your off-ice axels but just watched and I see the same issue with the off-ice axels as I mentioned with your axel on the ice. You are pulling in before you are in the proper rotational position and it's making you break at the waist and bring your left foot down. Before you pull in, you need to get backward with a straight right leg and *lifted left thigh*. You are keeping your left leg closed and straight, which is pulling you away from that axis you're supposed to be rotating over on the right side and also giving you nothing to pull in when it's time to pull in. So, again, Jump UP--leading with your right shin, get backward over the right hip with right leg straiight and left thigh lifted, THEN pull the arms and free leg in. And yes, you will need more height on the ice than on the floor because of the heavy skates and more open position.
