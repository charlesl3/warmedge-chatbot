Yes, I loved it! I was at a very low level and I got a lot of ice time. In my camp, there were very small groups but I think this was an anomaly because it was the first camp they offered at a new location. I went to the recommended hotel, very close to the rink and it was easy. We all had breakfast together and I walked with a coach to the rink. I paid extra for the stretch classes, they were amazing.

I really struggled with the off ice classes and skipped most of them. They were very challenging and I am injury prone (foot stress fractures). I went some days to the off ice classes and just did what I could or left early depending on how my body felt. I've been to 2 other camps since then and I would choose the Ryabinin camps as my next one if it lined up with my work schedule.

I took my daughter who was 10 last year to the one in Marianske Lazne. It was intense for her because it was her first experience with training for several hours/day. At the time she was working on her axel.

At first, she was overwhelmed, but after two weeks, she didn't want to leave. We saw the adult group, and there were several levels of all ages. The coaches were intense and their levels of English varied. Much of the learning happened like this: I show you, you do it. They also use a hands-on approach, much unlike Americans. This was something that my daughter had to adjust to. It is also no-nonsense, compared to her American training. There were no accolades for minor accomplishments in fact, no one used her name for the first week. But when they started to call her by name the second week, boy, did she feel proud.

The value is excellent compared to training in the USA (at least what we are used to) and we enjoyed the Czech Republic so much. For me as a mom, it wasn't a break, because the day is structured with many breaks that I had to be present to feed her, help her, or hang out. It isn't like USA camps where you drop your kid off at 9 and pick them up at 3. If you have specific questions, please don't hesitate to ask.

She wants to go back this summer. I'm trying to figure out how to fund it so we can manage.

Ryabinin Camp Review

Since we struggled to find detailed reviews beforehand, we thought we’d share our experience attending the Ryabinin camp. For context, our family has a mixed American-European background—some of us skated in the Midwest before relocating to Western Europe, so we’re familiar with both skating environments.

The Good:
• Well-Organized Camp: The camp is generally well-run. However, be aware that you’ll only receive the schedule for Day 1 a few days before the camp starts. After that, the daily schedule is released just one day in advance.

• Comprehensive Daily Structure: Each day includes two on-ice sessions, one off-ice training session, and one dance/choreography class. This is more complete than what many other sub-Novice-level camps offer, where you might only get two on-ice and one off-ice session per day.

• High-Quality Off-Ice Training: The off-ice sessions are intense and well-designed, with a clear focus on jump training. They’re physically demanding and seem thoughtfully planned.

• English-Speaking Coaches: All coaches speak enough English to communicate effectively, especially when combined with visual demonstrations. Don’t expect in-depth discussions, but basic instruction works well.

• Hands-On Coaching Style: Coaches use a hands-on approach that helps skaters feel the correct technique—something especially helpful for younger or more tactile learners.

• Lovely Atmosphere: Most if not all parents and other skaters we met are super friendly. Lots of nice rink side chats amongst the parents. The facility in Czech Republic is very nice, exceeding our expectations.

The Not-So-Good:
• Crowded Ice Sessions: Depending on registration numbers, you might end up with 30+ skaters on the ice at the same time. These are not beginners, but include skaters already landing doubles. This crowding makes it nearly impossible to work effectively on skating skills or step sequences that require space.

• Adults Mixed with Kids: Adults are on the ice together with children, which in our opinion poses a safety risk. Adults have a different field of vision and may not always see smaller children, especially when jumping or spinning. Remember, there are are already 30
skaters on the ice and then you mix in adults with 110cm kids on an already crowded ice.

• Large Group Sizes: Some groups may have up to 12 skaters per coach. While the coaches do make an effort to correct everyone, time per skater is inevitably limited compared to camps with smaller capped groups.

• Expensive Private Lessons: Private lessons cost €130, plus an additional €20 for ice time—among the highest we’ve encountered in Europe.

• Extra Ice Add-Ons: Optional extra ice sessions are offered at €50 per day. Quality varies depending on group size—some had only 4 skaters, others had 7–8. Although the camp advertises that harness use is possible, we did not see a single harness used all week. With large groups, it seems unlikely.

• Mixed Skill Levels in Groups: While groups are technically based on skill level, the group sizes are simply too large, which results in a wide range of abilities within the same group. For example, in a “beginner” group, there were skaters who had never attempted a waltz jump alongside others already landing loops and are leaning 1F and 1Lz. In more advanced groups, those working on refining their axels (good success rate) were grouped with skaters who were still trying to land one, making focused technical work more difficult.

• Off-Ice and Dance Grouping by Age Only: Both off-ice and dance/choreography sessions are divided by age alone, not by skill. This led to situations where 5-year-old beginners were grouped with 8-year-olds already landing axels.


All in all, will we come back? At the moment, no. We will try other camps with pre-defined group levels and a guarantee maximum group size. If we don't find anything better, then yes, we might come back.

Hi! You can try the following:

Jorik Hendrickx's camp: Bootcamp on ice Held in Deurne, Belgium. He's got it set up for multiple skill levels and there are many other coaches and choreographers working to help at his camp this year like Adam Solya, Agnes Zawadzki, Marina Shirskova, etc.

Benoit Richaud's Peak Ice Camp is happening in Italy and France this summer. It looks to be multi-skill levels.

Michael Huth's iceDOME camp is all levels from beginner-up..He's got an excellent mix of coaches with him including Nicole Schott, Stanick Jannette, Vanessa Gusmeroli and more. It's usually held in two cities: Oberstdorf and another.

IcePlanet by Neil Brown will have summer camp this year in Canillo, Andorra. 29 June-26 July, 2025.

Javier Fernandez has a camp as well. There isn't much info right now, but you could check on it.

Ice-Lab in Italy also has summer camps for multiple skill levels in Bergamo and Milan.

Good luck! I hope this works out for you both and you come back to tell us which camp(s) you chose and how they went!
