Why are sit spins so hard for adults? Is it our height? Do we lack strength?
I'm talking about healthy-weight adults who aren't afraid of falling, but we just can't get down low enough!

1. Do you think it requires more strength to get lower if you are taller?
2. Do adults lack strength because they are older or because they are taller?

I've been told that sit spins are easier if you go past 90degrees and sink lower. And because this position is easier, it doesn't "count" as a real sit spin during tests?

I don't know how a lower-than-90-degrees sit spin would be any easier. All of it is hard to me. I can put my butt all the way down to my ankles if I'm on two-legs, but I cannot do a shoot-the-duck on one leg. One leg does not have the strength that two legs do. And I'm pretty sure my right leg (dominant) is a little bit stronger than my left leg because my back spins are slightly lower.

My normal sit spin is well-balanced and lasts a long time, but it's the typical "way-too-high-adult-sit-spin".

I feel like I'm doing this: http://people.bath.ac.uk/chsgk/pictures/sitspin.jpg
but in reality I'm doing this: https://sarahonice.files.wordpress.com/2013/11/sit1113-2.jpg

This is why I don't film myself too often. Because it's completely discouraging to see what "feels awesome" actually looks really pathetic.


UPDATE!!

Figured out a new way that massively improved how low I can go in the spin. I was doing two-foot dip spins, just trying to see how low I could go in a spin if using two feet, and that helped me figure out a better position for my sit spin. I also found that putting my left hand on my left leg helped me sink down a bit further. It's like having that hand there reminds me to keep trying to get lower. I still keep the right arm stretched out in front of me.
http://i115.photobucket.com/albums/n307/Arwen17evenstar/sit-spin-improvement.jpg
I'm not quite 90 degrees, but very close now.

The bigger your body is--and the less flexible--the harder a low sit spin will be. Having said that, I can tell you that my issue with the sit spin did NOT turn out to be lack of strength, it turned out to be mediocre coaching. My first coach told me to get lower but never told me HOW to get lower or how I could tell when I was low enough. I had my knees together on the spin and when I tried to go lower, I would just fall back and sit down.

I switched coaches and my new coach told me I needed to feel the lower calf of my spinning leg pressing against the inner thigh of my free leg (note: you cannot do this with your knees together!). So I did a few backward shoot-the-ducks with my skating heel lifted (to simulate a sit spin but without the spin) and I practiced lowering my free leg and pushing my chest and free leg forward until I could feel what he described. By the end of our lesson, my sit spin was low enough. You can practice getting into this position off-ice as well. Just make sure you wear shoes with about a 2" heel so that your heel is lifted off the floor and you are on the ball of your foot like you are during a sit spin. The balance for simulating a sit spin position is different from doing them flat-footed.

Here are a few more technique tips to help you get lower:
(1) Get as low as you can on the entrance edge. You should feel like your skating thigh is parallel to the ice (It won't be, but it should feel like it). Also get a deep knee bend on the preparatory edge BEFORE the entrance edge (on your right leg, assuming you are about to do a forward sit on your left). The progression should be low--lower--lowest.
(2) Keep your back arched, tight and upright, not rounded or pitched forward. Do this from the moment you step down onto the entrance edge until you stand up out of the sit spin.
(3) Extend (and point) your free leg as far behind you as possible on the entrance edge. That will not only give you more momentum when you bring it to the front (you'll be creating a wider arc), but it will also put your free leg farther forward when it gets to the front, which will keep your weight forward on the blade so you can sit lower without falling back.

I think in most cases it doesn't have anything to do with height or age, but simply strength and flexibility.

Can you squat down with one leg while holding the other one out in front of you in the sit spin position off ice in a slow and controlled manner both up and down? If you can do that for at least 5 reps while not holding onto anything, then you definitely have the strength to do a proper sit spin on ice.

I would recommend watching this video https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=I8bo2InRYI4
I would work on this movement with both legs. Once you have mastered the movement and are able to go all the way down off ice without holding on to anything, your sit-spin should improve drastically.

Remember that after a certain level skating is very much a strength sport. Off ice training is very important to master some of the more difficult movements on ice, and proper off ice training will speed up your progress on ice by a significant margin.

Even the first sit spin picture you posted I think is kinda borderline for IJS. It could just be the angle but it looks like the thigh might not be parallel to the ice, and the butt is higher than the knee. Maybe some experts could chime in?

I do think that spins are easier to learn if you're little, but I have seen a lot of great, low, fast, sit spins from adults. One of the lowest I've ever seen was a lady in her 50's which motivated me so much to see. I think you're right that strength/size ratio has a lot to do with it. You have to have the strength to both squat down and get yourself up from a lower position on one leg, as well as the strength to hold up the free leg to keep it from hitting the ice, once you get low enough. One leg squats off ice can help a lot.

I'm an adult skater with a standard 9-5 job so I understand having to pick and choose what to do in a given day.

I go to the gym 3 days, skate 3 days, with 1 day completely off. That probably works out to about 6 hours of gym time and 12 of actual skate time during a week long period. I skate at a relatively small rink that doesn't have a lot of skate times after 5pm unfortunately, so its hard for me to get a lot of time in during the week. Most of my time spent there is on the weekends.

Its hard to say how many times you should go to the gym vs the rink because each person is different and has to tailor their routine to their schedule and individual goals.

As a start I would shoot for a minimum of 30 minutes of off ice training 3 days a week, and then fill the rest with as much skate time as you can. The off ice time would include the strength training like the pistol squat, and then also a stretching routine and off ice jump practice ideally.

I'm kinda mean when teaching sit spins because of how I learned them. You have to force yourself to go lower and lower.
And if that means falling, well, you're close to the ice so just let go and take the fall.

At some point just before you fall you'll feel maybe one rotation that is balanced for as low as you want to go, just before you fall. Eventually you'll have two rotations, then three, etc.

I get that this is much harder for adults but I see kids with half-a**ed sit spins too and there is no excuse for that. My bottom had many a soggy session when I was learning them.

Stretch to help with your flexibility, especially the lower back area. Good luck.

One often overlooked point is the ankles. In the first picture, the skater's ankle is strongly flexed, allowing the knee to bend and push forward beyond the skating toes. In the second picture, the ankle is almost 90 degrees, making it impossible to get "low".

Remember, when someone tells you to bend your knees, they really mean, bend your ankles!
