I was told that as long as you are making progress to stick with your current coach. When the progress stops, it is time to switch. It sounds like you are making progress so I don't see the hurry either.

In my skating club, they publish all the test session online so it is a matter of getting the coach's ok before you sign up. I have always sign up my daughter for each test session, not the coach.

Are you talking pre-pre MIF or freeskate? If you want to compete and going to follow the "test track," then you don't take the preliminary freeskate until you have a solid axel. The skills that are required on the test do not mesh with the skills you need to preform in a competition. For instance my daughter skater at the Juvenile level but she could pass the Novice freeskate test (and on a good day probably even the Junior one). Remember once you test, you can no longer compete at the lower levels.

You indeed seem under 18, since you mentioned the parents having the talk. I have never changed coaches, since there is only one in my city (yes, one) and I just started skating last month, but I do have some experience with changes. Here are two suggestions:

If you are progressing quickly, I'm not sure about the rush either. Usually skaters have very solid reasons for quitting a coach (the coach is consistently late or cancels for example). I am reading your post and can't understand the reason other than there is a different coach you want because they have a more impressive roster of students.

In this case, the person paying for lessons should explain the reason face to face with the coach. If a young child is involved, I would hesitate at quitting, they might really like and feel safe with their current coach, you would need to explain it so they understand why they need a coach in a way they can feel okay about the change. I would have a thank you note/gift for the last lesson.

It may be very awkward for a very long time around the coach you let go. That is just a fact when you break off a relationship without clear reasons. Or the coach might not care at all and have been considering when to ask you to move on. You just won't know until it happens.

1) Most skaters have more than the skills required to pass a test. A lot of coaches have an idea of when the "right time" to move up is skill-wise. In my area, Axels are an absolute minimum to compete Pre-pre standard track and at least two of double Sal/toe/loop to compete in Preliminary. It could be that your coach has a similar thought or similar experiences and so has not had you sign up to test yet OR was not at the rink before the close date/# of skaters of a test session. I know skaters who did not get signed up on the first day of a test session get closed out because it was full almost immediately, especially around Regionals or synchro requirements deadlines.

2) In my area, high level coaches won't take on a student who isn't at least at the Juvenile competitive level. That means a full set of doubles minus double Axel and serious progress on double Axel before you will even get a look or interest from these coaches. Have you set your mind on a new coach and is he/she taking on new students and at what level?

If the coach hasn't had higher level skaters because he hasn't been coaching long, then I would want to look at what the coach's own skating experience is. How far did he get himself in testing or competing? Have you seen him skate? Does/did he seem to have good technique?

If you're going to be working on double jumps, even if your coach is new to teaching them because he hasn't yet had a student at that level, then do you believe he understands the technique because he has done good double jumps himself?

It is the job of the coach to understand the test and competition structures and to advise skaters when to test. Or, rather to consult with skaters and their parents about what their goals are, explain options, and advise the best strategy to achieve those goals.

If the coach isn't yet very familiar with the options or the standards for tests and competitions, do they seem on the ball to learn what they need to know by the time the skater needs to know it? Do they have more experienced coaches they can ask for advice?

Has this coach taken other skaters through moves tests higher than pre-preliminary, and do they usually seem to pass?

There are differences between 1) low-level coaches who teach only beginners, 2) local coaches who can take recreational skaters through middle levels, 3) local coaches who have the technical knowledge to develop strong mid-level (i.e., juvenile/intermediate) competitors or take semi-serious skaters through all their tests but may need more experience (with ambitious skaters) and resources to negotiate the system to get students to that level, and 4) elite coaches who can take talented ambitious skaters to successful competition at novice level and above. Even the latter had to start out somewhere.

I don't know this guy, and neither do any of the other posters here, so I wouldn't assume one way or another. I certainly would not jump to the conclusion that you're looking to jump straight to a category 4) elite-level coach.

You're the one who knows your current coach and who else is available to you at your rink. If you think your coach really only has the knowledge to fit into the 1) or 2) category and you think you need someone in category 2) or 3) to achieve your goals, then you should switch, and the coach should understand why you're moving on if you explain your reasons. If you think he already has the technical knowledge from his own skating and a desire to grow in his coaching knowledge/experience with more time in the job, and if you have a good rapport, then you should stay with him.
