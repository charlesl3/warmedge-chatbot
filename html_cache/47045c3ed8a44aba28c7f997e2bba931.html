I'm so sorry, you all get so many of these. I'm just glad I found a nice place with such helpful feedback! I figured I'd go ahead and ask, just for some general and helpful advice.

I'm trying to set some general advice on basic goals for a beginner skater in learning the basic of all basic jumps.

I'm newly 28, skating for recreation. I've been able to skate (public skate sessions, etc.) since I was 6 but I actually didn't start getting serious about it until October. I go 2-3 times a week on average, usually anywhere between 3 and 7 hours depending on sessions. I have a friend who's been helping teach me things since he used to skate, so I've learned forwards/backwards crossovers, two-foot spins, swizzles, slaloms, most of the basics really. (We haven't learned a thing in order, mostly because I pick up things oddly.) Right now we're on 3-turns and that's my current challenge.

I'm not in any rush to learn, but I want to set a goal on when either he can or a coach at the rinks can teach me a starter jump. With consistent practice, does a year in sound like a reasonable goal? Nine months? I was hoping to try and learn by summer, but I'm starting to wonder if maybe going for fall would be more logical, etc. My friend says I shouldn't have a problem picking it up by September, but I just wasn't sure. Advice or guidance? Thanks!

Seconding jf12's points.

Also, most rinks allow beginner private lessons on public sessions -- though you're better off on the less crowded ones.

The point is that you'll be better off learning these skills from a coach who is experienced in coaching, rather than from a friend who can do the skills himself but may not have any experience or theoretical knowledge about how to teach them. The advantage of private lessons over group lessons is that the coach can work directly with the skills you already have and address your personal problem areas rather than planning lessons to be appropriate for a group of people with different strong and weak points.

I don't know about your friend's experience but I did see a little red flag when you said he's teaching things sort of our of order. It might be fine, but some of the things that are taught in the beginner levels are important skills that might seems silly but are teaching you the basics you will need for higher level skills. It's important to get these basics (in my opinion) so you are prepared for the harder skills and to avoid picking up bad habits. That is one of the reasons that those lesson structure exists and why students are supposed to master the elements at the lower levels before moving up. When my daughter was little I remember being frustrated that she was held back and had to repeat a level and the the instructor explained to me that she just wasn't strong enough in a particular skill that was an important one that she'd need later. We also had an experience at a rink where they had her skip some levels and she ended up never being taught a lot of the half jumps. It didn't seem like a big deal at the time but since she skipped some of the basic entry techniques for those jumps she picked up some bad habits that took a LONG time to correct.

Sounds like you are in the US, but in Canada at a Skate Canada club [as opposed to public skating] there is a strict prohibition about anyone giving instruction who is not a Skate Canada nationally certified coach at the appropriate level. Parents or friends giving suggestions from the stands or boards is a strict no, let alone on ice. It's all part of the liability policy and insurance coverage. But sometimes we do see young skaters who share a session at an equivalent level showing each other things and challenging each other in their independent time.
