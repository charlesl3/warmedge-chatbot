There's a difference between "safe" and "wise." Plenty of people manage to figure out how to do something simple like skate forward on their own without killing themselves, obviously, but they usually don't do it very well. For my part, I started skating in the spring and lessons weren't available until the fall (logistics of living in a rural area). I hadn't really skated before so I went through USFS's Basic 1 and 2 for a few weeks just to give myself familiarity with it (aka, I got up to a one foot glide forward, so not very far at ALL). It did help when I was able to take lessons, but there were things I needed to fix of course. I should also point out that as a dance teacher I'm quite familiar with teaching beginning students so I'm pretty savvy as to taking care of myself and what's a good idea/what's not for me and my body.

So if your question is "how many tricks can I learn by myself," I'm with karne; the answer is none, full stop. If your question is "Can I go to a public skating session and do a few marches or two foot glides so when I start lessons I can be on the ice without hanging onto the wall," then that's okay (remember you're not really teaching yourself, you're dabbling, and you should expect that the longer you dabble the more bad habits you'll pick up). If the word "trick" enters your vocabulary that's, IMO, dangerous--tricks to me means spins and jumps and you should not attempt to learn those things without supervision.

To remain safe, keep both feet on the ice at all times, and bend your knees.

But just in case, the first thing to learn is how to fall and get up again safely.

You could follow Princessroja's lead by looking up the skills taught in the first few levels of learn-to-skate lessons:
https://learntoskateusa.com/media/1164/curriculum_basicskills.pdf

One advantage these days is that you could search for each of the skills by name on youtube and probably find a video of a professional demonstrating and explaining the correct way to do it.

Take your time, follow instructions for the Basic 1 skills and get comfortable with them before trying to learn any from Basic 2, and the same for Basic 2 before moving on to Basic 3.

Don't expect to get past the Basic 3 skills on your own.

You'll notice that the Basic 3 class includes a "bonus skill" of forward pivots.
That's the closest thing to a "trick" that might be safe to self-teach with video instruction.
https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sgVO6WXqHsU

Possibly you could move from there to a two-foot spin: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=U2YZXZ2LXDI

But by the time you're ready to start trying those moves, you should also be ready to learn how to hold edges and do crossovers, forward and then backward. You really should get in-person instruction on those skills. And you will need to be pretty secure on those skills without bad habits before you move on to anything resembling freeskating.

To start with, the best thing would be just spending some time on the ice, with both feet and bent knees, finding your balance and getting the feel of gliding. That's what will be most helpful to you when you start real lessons, even though it's all about internal proprioception and not something you can show off as a trick. Feeling how your weight is balanced over the blades is something that will serve you well for all more advanced skills that an instructor can teach you when you're ready.

A true figure skating mohawk is a change from one foot to the other foot, on foot on the ice at a time, on edges. The easiest mohawk is forward inside to backward inside in your good direction. You wouldn't be able to learn it before you're able to hold a backward inside edge on one foot and also control the check of your upper body before and after the turn. You won't be ready for that before you learn crossovers and edges.

If you're starting to work on backward skating, you'll be transitioning on two feet between going forward and going backward. If you keep your feet parallel to each other on the ice while you turn from forward to backward, that would simulate a three turn on both feet.

If you turn out your feet so that one is traveling forward and the other traveling backward on the ice at the same time, that would be more like a mohawk or a little inside spread eagle -- it seems that's what hockey players call a mohawk. If your hips naturally turn out easily, that kind of turn might be just as easy for you as the parallel-feet two-foot turn. If your hips don't like to turn out, it'll be a lot harder for you. And even if it does come easily to you, developing muscle memory for a turned-out two-foot turn probably wouldn't help and might hurt your ability to learn real figure skating mohawks when you're ready.


Work on one-foot glides. At first you may barely be able to pick up one foot before you need to put it down again for balance. But when you get to the point that you can glide on one foot for several seconds at a time, you could start to play with free leg positions and forward stroking from one foot to the other.

And then take some lessons! Start with one or two lessons to get some instruction on crossovers and edges, and then practice the correct technique that you learn on your own.

It sounds like you're very sensible to recognize that there are things you cannot learn without a coach. That said, sticking with skills you are currently doing, I would recommend you try to develop some basic body awareness, that will become helpful down the road when you (hopefully!) will have a coach. For example, where are your free leg, hips, shoulders and arms in relation to your body? Do you feel what part of the blade you are doing your one foot glides and edges (front near toe pick or middle or back)? Concepts such as shoulder placement and what part of the blade to use for what moves are essential for mastering harder skills, and you'll need good body awareness in order to learn those skills when the time comes. I'll let your future coach teach you the correct body/blade placements, but it's never too soon to develop body awareness on your own. It's not really something a coach can teach easily.

How are you defining"safe"? When it comes to skating, I, define safety by the level of probability that I will break a bone doing whatever it is.

I thought standing still in one spot in skates on the ice was "safe", until a slight turn of my foot made me fall on my leg, resulting in two spiral fractures, almost a week in the hospital, and two surgeries! I broke a wrist just doing a little back mohawk. However, never once have I broken or sprained anything doing a jump or spin. This has led me to conclude that jumping and spining are no less safe than standing still.

A coach can teach you proper way to fall and get back up, and can teach you proper positions and techniques so that you are less likely to fall. A coach can call an ambulance if you break a bone, cut yourself, or get a head injury from falling during a lesson, and would have liability insurance in case you try to sue him or her for your getting injured during a lesson.

The best thing you can do is not try anything that you haven't been shown and had explained to you in person, by someone who has a lot of experience doing it.

One thing that is safe for you to do on your own is to learn skating terminology and concepts. The different parts of the blade, what edges are, what "free hip" means, etc. are things that you can learn on your own and can be done in complete safety. This will make it easier for you to pick up on what the coach says if/when you take lessons.

Click to expand...

It depends on your finances, how often you practice, and how quickly you want to improve. For me, my lessons with my coach average less than once a month, because 1) I need a lot of practice time to learn skills as an adult 2) she packs a lot of information into each lesson and 3) after each lesson, I spend the next hour or two writing up everything she said I needed to work on, so I end up with a full page of things to work on for the next month (or two). So having more lessons than that is a bit redundant. It depends on whether or not you can find a coach that's willing to give lessons irregularly though, and whether or not you're the type to analyze your own skating and know what you need to work on on your own.

If you are recently employed, you might consider starting with private lessons once every week or every two weeks first, or just go for group lessons (which are cheaper), before considering if you really want or need private lessons multiple times a week. I think you'll improve faster that way rather than going for a "one lesson a month now, then 3 or more lesson a week when I'm rolling in dough" sort of strategy -- I learned more in my first month of group lessons than all the times messing around on my own before them, so I think having a few lessons to start off will really help.
