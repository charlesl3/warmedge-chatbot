Agreeing with both of you, and just expanding on this. I, too, find that YouTube can be helpful for ice dance, granted that you are already taking lessons from a qualified coach. Much like Scout, I have weekly lessons where my coach shows me the dances and makes sure that I'm learning correctly and safely. When I'm at home, I like to watch YouTube videos to help memorize the pattern and timing of each dance, as silver.blades suggested. This saves valuable time in lessons and lets me fully internalize each dance, building upon what I've learned in person. I would not recommend learning anything from scratch from YouTube ever - it is merely a tool to help cement steps or patterns in your mind after you've been taught how to properly execute it by a coach. (And have a coach monitor you frequently to make sure you're not changing your behavior.)

For ice dance specifically, Oleg Altukhov (Senior-level ice dancer in the US) has helpful YouTube videos that break down compulsory dances and moves.

I knew everyone would be against self-teaching via youtube, but I am still annoyed that everyone is so critical of it.

As a beginner I’ve learned/taught myself many things via youtube videos or just by observing and analysing video footage of others skating (namely: back and forward crossovers, mohawks, back 3-turns, single jumps up to loop/partly flip, choctaws and a few counters, rockers and brackets, so I’d say quite a variety of skills). And I still do.
I did it out of curiosity and because I was eager to learn about new things (and not necessarily always with the goal of trying it myself), and because I was interested in learning certain things that we hadn’t done yet during (group) lessons, that I felt ready for and that I was interested in specifically.

And I’ve never gotten bad feedback on my ‚self-taught’ technique/execution from any coach, quite the contrary actually (now that of course could be a reflection on my coaches’ qualities , and let’s just say that some of them are certainly not the most competent coaches out there , but since these are very basic skills, I trust that they will spot the most apparent mistakes and can tell when something is done in a wrong way).

I’m not saying that everything I do is a 100 % correct (and obviously I am still working on and constantly trying to improve everything) or that I wouldn’t have learned these things faster if there had been a coach giving me constant feedback/corrections while I was working on new things (but where would I get such a coach, that would observe me around the clock, never mind the costs).

But honestly, not trying to brag or put others down, but most of my fellow skaters, who don’t seek out videos/tutorials and don’t try to educate themselves via the internet and are strictly following what they’re getting taught during lessons, are doing a lot worse than me, and it takes them much longer to do certain skills successfully (i.e. on an acceptable level), if at all.

I also don’t think that ‚forming bad habits’ is that much of a risk.
When I watch the skaters in my hometown, no matter which level/background/age, all of them have bad habits or weaknesses, that even I can spot immediately. Lessons with a coach are no guarantee that a skater won’t develop bad habits anyway, and not all bad habits get ‚caught’ by a coach (especially when a skater is only/mostly doing group lessons, but also out of laziness or lack of motivation on the coach’s part, or because they have low expectations of a skater and don’t bother correcting everything).
And even if a bad habit gets noticed/caught in time, there’s no guarantee a skater will correct it, or it may take them quite some time to do so. (it’s also possible to notice a bad habit/mistake yourself and try to get rid of it)

And I’m also asking myself…. who cares? So what if some people choose to teach themselves instead of taking lessons (or not as many lessons) and they end up with bad technique/a few bad habits, if they’re just doing it for fun, for recreational purposes?
I’m sure there are valid reasons (from their perspective) to forgo the help of a coach (I guess in most cases availability of coaching or lack of money).
And I also assume it’s mostly adults trying to self-teach through videos and they won’t be going up to higher levels anyway, so what’s the harm?
If, for lack of alternatives, they’re ok with progressing slower than others, because they don’t have the assistance of an experienced teacher, and if it’s fine with them if their execution isn’t the best?

Even before youtube, I remember seeing some young men about 20 years ago attempting to teach themselves beginning jumps and spins based on what they had seen on TV and likely videotapes.

But they hadn't bothered to learn how to control edges first. Attempting single toe loops and salchows without being able to control a simple backward edge was pretty scary to watch and not something you want to be next to when the person is trying it.

One thing that lessons are useful for is teaching skills in a logical sequence, so that skills build on each other and so you don't attempt tempting moves you think you're ready for before you first master the prerequisite skills beforehand.

If you're wise, you'll focus on learning basics first before more advanced or flashier skills. You can look up standard sequences of skill learning online and get a sense of what you need to learn first.

But that's just one part of it.

Many of the techniques rely on hard-won knowledge (developed over more than a century of trial and error and refinement) not only of the best positions for arms or head or free leg on executing various edges and turns, as well as jumps and spins, but also subtler techniques about which muscle groups to tense or relax at what part of the move. You can see an arm position or leg position when you watch another skater, up close or on video, and try to copy it. You can't usually see when they're pointing or relaxing the toes inside the boot or contracting the back muscles or abdominals or buttocks, or when they're leaning over an edge from the hip down or knee down or ankle down.

A youtube coach may or may not mention these details in passing, but they probably won't go into a lot of detail about exactly which muscles to activate when. They're only going to give one lesson on the same move, which will usually be more or less of an overview of how to do it. Even if they give the best information in the world, they only give you one video's worth about that move. If you practice the move for a while and then come back for more feedback, you're only going to get the same information again from the same video.

An in-person lesson, especially a private lesson from a strong technical coach, will be able to give more detailed information, including by putting hands on your body to help you feel things, rather than relying only on what can be seen on video. And they can give feedback and corrections on what you're doing right and what you're doing wrong so you can make corrections. You might need completely different corrections from another skater following the same instructions with different results. And you might need completely different corrections next week than what you needed this week.

One of the most important skills it's important to learn when making the transition from basic recreational straight-line skating to figure skating is how to "check" rotation on an edge, during crossovers, and later on threes and other turns, jumps, etc.

Does anyone know of videos that address how to do this in a way that can make sense to the learner at a distance?

Before there were videos, there were how-to books, in which experts would offer a lot of words and a few pictures to try to teach beginners how to do beginning skills, and sometimes included a selection of more advanced skills as well. I don't know if anyone successfully taught themselves to do these skills from books alone.

Most of the books I'm thinking of date from the figures era. Has anyone been able to master a forward circle eight, for example, or even nice controlled half circles, with only some verbal and visual instruction?

It may be easier for skaters who have already mastered the basics in depth with advanced understanding of the principles of how skating moves work in their bodies to learn something else from that kind of instruction that draws on what they already know. But a beginner doesn't know what they don't know.

I’m not really making the claim that it’s as safe as skating with a coach. I was saying that claiming it’s more dangerous requires proof. I don’t think there’s much proof because hardly anyone self-teaches like that. Most take classes or private lessons.

And I’m not talking about outlandishly dangerous coaches. I’m talking about coaches that don’t teach good technique or coaches who neglect teaching important things like the rules of freestyle.

My main point is that it’s not just that coaching is better than self teaching but rather that the best way to learn is from a good coach. Emphasis on good.
