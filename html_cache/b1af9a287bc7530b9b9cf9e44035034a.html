Doing a back upright spin with your leg behind you is not really a relevant skill for back camel, which is not an upright spin -- the upper body position is just as much a part of it as the free leg and they have to balance each other.

Also, you shouldn't "throw" your leg into position.

I first learned back camel from a change of foot from forward camel, with the upper body staying in position and just the feet changing. So you'd need to be able to get a few revolutions of forward camel first. The forward inside three entry and flying camel came later. I never got very good at it, though.

We can't describe to you in words exactly what's necessary, any more than you can describe to us in words exactly what you're doing that isn't working.

That's why working with a coach who can see you and touch you and offer immediate corrections would be much more useful.

Failing that, see if you can find videos of coaches demonstrating and explaining, or slow-motion of skaters a bit more advanced than you who do the move as well as you're aiming for (not elite skaters who might bypass or vary some of the steps you need to focus on, and not beginners still struggling with it themselves), and see what they're actually doing with their shoulders, hips, arms, free leg, timing of turn from entry edge or change of foot from forward camel, etc.

What's hard to see is what muscles to engage when or how far forward or back your weight should be over the blade and when it may need to shift. That's where an in-person instructor can help in ways that neither verbal explanations nor video can really convey.

Has your coach taught you back camel at all? It's probably not something you can teach yourself from scratch. Even 5 minutes of lesson on it will give you better techniques to practice than what we can offer.

That said, your best bet is probably to try doing a forward camel, keep your upper body parallel to the ice, and change feet the same way you would in any change-foot spin.

For the FI three turn entry, watch mid-level skaters doing this spin and see where their arms and shoulders are on the forward inside edge and what they change in the arms/shoulders and free leg/hips at the point of the turn. Again, keep the upper body down, don't try to start the spin with an upright torso.

Even though the entry edge is the same for back camel and back scratch (RFI, for CCW spinners), the body position and arm/leg motions on the entry edge are completely different. This is the same situation for the forward camel and forward scratch: the entries are the same (LFO) but the body and arm/leg positions are different. I assume you would know this, since you can do forward camel and forward scratch.

gkelly's point is that different people find it easier to learn back camel from different entries: RFI entry, change foot entry, flying entry, etc. The different entries require different body positions and arm/leg movements. Personally, I find change foot entry easier, because I'm already spinning and I simply shift my sweet spot from L to R foot; however, tricky thing is keeping the upper body still and learning the correct free leg movement. RFI entry involves a different set of correct arm movements and and body position, plus learning how to initiate the rotation.

Bottom line is, try different entries, and practice the one that works best for you, before attempting other entries. You should focus on learning the correct technique, thereafter finding your sweet spot will come naturally. Don't spent time worrying about sweet spots before you've learnt the correct technique.

That is not true. Independent practice is very much part of skating culture.

A general rule of thumb is to practice (without supervision) about twice as much time on your own as you spend in a lesson.

The point is that you should be practicing techniques that you have already learned from someone who has years of experience doing and teaching what works. You shouldn't be trying to reinvent the wheel yourself by guessing what you're supposed to be doing just by observing from afar.


Oaksmith said:
Then there is this idea that you will learn bad habits. Where is the evidence to support these statements? I have a bachelors and master's in Kinesiology.
Click to expand...
In that case you will likely have a better understanding of body movement in general than the average person off the street who steps onto the ice and wants to try things without instruction.

You will probably make better progress on your own between lessons that someone without that background. Maybe you would do well with 4 or 5 times as many practice hours as lesson hours. Most beginners would not.

But again, you shouldn't have to guess what to do with your body to make your blades do what you want them to. Get input from experts from what has been proven to work, and get feedback from an expert eye on what you're doing and how it's working.

By all means practice on your own. But make sure you understand what good technique is for the skills you're working on rather than trying to figure it out from scratch. Make sure you develop the prerequisite blade skills before you start to embellish with flashy tricks.

I have occasionally seen young adult skaters on the ice trying to teach themselves jumps -- even double jumps -- without having mastered the ability to control a simple edge or three turn. That is a recipe for disaster, unsafe not only for the person making the attempt but also for others on the ice nearby. Learning by "messing up a lot" while traveling at speed on a slippery surface with knives on your feet is what can lead to injuries -- and sometimes to innocent bystanders.

If you understand the general progression of skills and have access to input on how to control edges, how to execute basic turns and control the exit edges, how to generate power efficiently, how to use your arm/head/shoulder positions and which muscles to tighten or relax for which skills, etc., then you can spend time on your own practicing how to put that knowledge into practice in your own body.

And if at all possible, try to find a coach who makes you feel good about yourself for mastering skills.

Trying to figuring stuff out on your own while intentionally ignoring the whole history of skating technique is a waste of time, when you could make much better progress by learning established technique and learning the building blocks from the bottom up.

How comfortable are you with forward to backwards change-foot spinning? Are you able to do a sit-change-sit?

Also, how is your spiral? Because if you have difficulty controlling a spiral on the foot of your back camel, it will be hard to do it while spinning.

Back camels are harder because you need more hip strength and strong glutes to hold your free leg up. As far as exercises go, you need to develop a strong lower back and hip flexibility.

Superman exercises can help develop your back/glutes/hip strength, where you lie on your stomach and lift your outstretched feet up individually or simultaneously like in this video: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=z6PJMT2y8GQ

Also off-icebarre/rink board exercise: lift up your camel leg and hold for 5-10 seconds and then when it's just starting to burn, lift it just a little higher for 5 more seconds. This will develop the glute and allow you to hold it in a spin.

edit: Oh I just saw this was a past post and you have your back camel - nice! For the flying camel the above exercises are still applicable, but the difference of course is you need a slight knee bend/push up/lock your leg when you land on your spinning foot and also really important to have a strong back/glutes.
