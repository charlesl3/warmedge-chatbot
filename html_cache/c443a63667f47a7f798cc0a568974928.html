Its because of statistics. Statistically performance peak in figure skating for girls coming somewhere between 16th-18/19th year, so its very probably that 14 years old girl will skate quite better than 12 years old girl, so its rational to let her compete against younger girls where she have bigger chance to win, than against +- same developed girls. BUT its all statistics, not rule! If you watching currently ongoing junior grand prix, there are 16, 17 years old girls which performance is terrible in comparison which much younger girls from Russia, South Korea or Japan etc. The key is training here. If girl have inpropper, inconsistent, poor, amateur training, even as an older (physically and maybe mentally stronger and more experienced) than younger girls, her potential will not develope and she will be incompetitive against propperly trained younger girls. So, as I wrote, older girl should have statistically advantage over younger girl, but there ares Trusovas, Scherbakovas, Kanishevas, Tarakanovas and many others which are teens or even preteens but can easily dominate over maybe 95% of other women skaters.

With ice dancers its more or less same, since also there you need to reach some level of psychological/emotional development to properly transfer emotions into dance. Maybe its even more important than in singles, but on the other side, its true that body changes in puberty probably havent so strong effect on performance than in singles.

Based on years of observation and some reading, I'd say

Jumping ability depends on how high the skater can jump, how quickly she can rotate, and how well she can control the edges in and out of the jump and control her body position in the air.

Jump height depends on the strength-to-weight ratio, which for girls tends to peak by the beginning of puberty, and also on the percentage of fast-twitch muscles, which is largely genetic but can be trained. Larger muscles after the growth spurt make for more strength/power, but for girls the increase in strength tends to be less than the increase in weight (larger muscles, which is good, but also larger bones and higher body fat ratio). Of course, if a girl starts strength training at the same time that her body starts developing, then she might gain more strength proportionally.

A smaller skater will be able to rotate more quickly than a larger one -- a larger circumference takes longer to get around, and long arms or legs are harder to control and to pull in tight. A curvy figure is less aerodynamic than a straight one.

Skating technique, and jumping technique specifically, improves the longer and more often the skater trains, assuming she's getting good instruction. And the mental understanding of the technique will increase with more maturity, so a 14-year-old who has been skating X hours a week for 5 years will probably have better technique than a 12-year-old who has been skating and taking lessons with the same frequency for the same length of time. If they both started at the same age, obviously the 14-year-old would be more advanced.

Everyone will lose some coordination when they're going through growth spurts and their body is changing rapidly. Usually patience will get them through that if they keep training, although there may be months or even a couple of years when they seem to regress. Boys' strength tends to increase more than their weight and their body shape tends to remain more straight up and down, so most boys will end up better jumpers after puberty than before. The best female jumpers will be those whose strength+technique increases more after puberty than their body size and shape changed -- small, muscular, not too curvy, and old enough to have trained for more years, mastered the mid-level skills and moved on to learn and master the more advanced ones. Not all older girls will be able to maintain a body type that allows them to do all the jumps they could when they were younger let alone learn harder ones -- they should be able to jump higher, but they may not be able to rotate anywhere near as quickly.

Smaller skaters also tend to rotate faster in spins, and younger children also tend to be more flexible than teenagers. But again, training to develop flexibility can counteract the tendency to become less flexible with maturation, and more experience will generally result in better technique and better positions.

For basic skating skills older skaters tend to have all the advantages -- more experience and more understanding, and greater absolute strength/power (as opposed to strength-weight ratio) that comes with larger muscles and longer legs.

And for presentation skills, the experience and mental + emotional understanding will also only tend to improve as the skater matures.

So in an event where the number of times each skater can rotate in the air determines the winners, the smaller-but-older skaters will tend to do best and younger ones may have an advantage over older ones just in terms of getting the harder jumps done consistently, or at all.

In events where basic skating skill and music interpretation and general poise and confidence are most important, older skaters will tend to do better.


Then there are also some social factors to take into account:

*Teenagers may become more self-conscious which could inhibit their ability to perform confidently to audiences.
*Teenagers who struggle with jumps as their body changes, or who recognize intellectually now that they're never going to be as successful as they once dreamed they'd be, or for whom other priorities such as education or social lives become more important, may choose to quit skating or scale back their training and therefore become less competitive, rather than continue pushing forward to maximize their personal potential.
*Highly talented skaters who started skating committed to serious training at young ages will master advanced skills at younger ages than skaters who started a bit older or who train less or who have more average native talent. In that case, the young phenoms will often move up to the competition level that best matches their skill set (and allows them to include their full jump repertoire) even if they are younger and smaller than the average skaters at that level.

So the very best skaters at each level are often among the youngest. But they are a small minority of the skating community. Among the majority, the older skaters of average talent and average commitment may have more advantages over the younger ones.

And within any post-puberty age cohort the naturally smaller girls will tend to be better jumpers than the taller and/or curvier girls.

Disregarding what has been posted here already in the first reply: I'll be straight up with you and tell you that as a current professional and former elite (yes the skaters you see on tv), that not every single nor a good amount of female figure skaters careers end at late teens/early 20s. Many of us skate to late 20's at least competitive-wise. Training and conditioning are a huge factor in that. Also, jump life doesn't end after puberty. I had minimal issues when it came to loss of jump technique due to puberty, and it was easily corrected by a jump tech. My jumps were still high and powerful.

The Russian girls of today that you see competing Jr and Sr should not be considered in this algorithm because it is known that they are stunted in their growth, weight and puberty. Many former Russian skaters have stated this including Lipnitskaya.

As far as ice dance (I have done both), we can compete longer into our 30's because of no jumping, and many other factors.

As far as skating during menses, I have had painful ones and might miss a day of training when I was competitive. Otherwise, you put in product, take some approved pain meds, and move on with your training or competition.

I don't know much about the test ages, but there are adult competitions that people in their 50s and 60s and even older can compete in. Retirement from elite competition is usually in the teens, 20s, or 30s, but there are many factors in that. Many continue to skate in shows long after that.

Bigger girls do not have advantages. I had no problems with puberty, but then again, my only jumps were a waltz jump, mazurka, and single Salchow, and I was one of those teensy, Anna Shcherbakova-sized Russian girls who look like kids until they're 20. I don't know anything about Russian skaters' growth being stunted on purpose, but I assure you, plenty of us look like that with no intentional interference whatsoever, and yes, unfortunately for those without those genes, we do have advantages.

For periods, just a tampon and paracetamol/acetominophen. You shouldn't be out of commission for several days because of your period. That sounds like some kind of endocrine problem. My friend had that issue, and it turned out to be hypothyroidism. There's no reason to think the same will happen to your daughter, but if she sees it happening to you, she might think that that's how you're supposed to react to a period and end up doing it because of that.
