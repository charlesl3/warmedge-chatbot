Here’s my core dump on figure skating laces (not applicable to EDEA boots).


(1) Lace materials. Laces are fabricated from a variety of materials, including all-cotton, all-nylon, all-polyester, and blends (for example, cotton/poly, cotton/nylon, and poly/nylon). (a) All-cotton stays tied down the best and has the least tendency to loosen (due to its high surface friction). However, I do not recommend them because they have poor wet strength: a few drops of water on them, and they go ker-snap; and even if you manage to keep them dry, they tend to go ker-snap without any early warning signs. (b) All-nylon is relatively impervious to water and lasts the longest. However, I do not recommend them because they become untied easily and has the highest tendency to loosen (due to its low surface friction). (c) My favorite is the Riedell cotton/poly blend: stays tied down, tends not to loosen, good wet strength, and long life. Note: Riedell makes (or has made) laces in a variety of materials, including all-cotton and all-nylon; so be careful of the ones you buy. I also like the Jackson Elite poly laces (they came with my Jackson Elite suede boots). I prefer the Riedell over the Jackson because the Riedell has a narrower width (my Jackson boots have small hooks), a denser weave, and a harder finish.


(2) Tension. Many lacing instructions recommend that you vary the tension along the boot: loose at the front over the toes, tight along the instep and bottom of the ankle, and loose along the top of the ankle. Sounds great in principle, except there’s one big flaw. Unless you use highly specialized lacing techniques, in the course of skating, the tension will tend to even out: if one area is loose and one area is tight, the tension will tend to even out, with the areas that were previously tight now becoming looser. I recommend pulling the laces as snug as you can throughout. If some areas pinch, then adjust the boot or insert foam pads where needed, but don’t leave the lace loose in some areas. My Jacksons have almost 1/2" thick soft foam as a tongue liner, so I can snug up without pinching.


(3) Lacing pattern. (a) Judging from your photo, looks like I use the same lacing pattern as you along the eyelets; but I don’t lace twice around the hooks (with my Jacksons, that would not be possible anyway , since the hooks are too small for double lacing). (b) Some people recommend a lacing pattern that locks the lace in place. I tried it, but I don’t like it because you have to get it right the first time: if you get it wrong, you can’t cinch them up; you have to unlace and start all over. (c) I like a simple lacing pattern that I can cinch up: after the top eyelet, I wrap the laces around each other 3-5 times (the optimum number depends on the lace and the boot). The trick is to wrap them around enough times that when you remove your hands, the laces do not loosen. But you also want to be able to take up excess slack by tugging on them (if you wrap them around too many times, you can’t take up excess slack, so play around with the number of wraps). I then go back and snug up the laces and take up any excess slack. Once that is done, I lace around the hooks as snug as possible. You should wrap the lace from the top of the hook to the bottom of the hook (rather than from the bottom of the hook to the top of the hook) to maximize the contact area and reduce slippage. After the top hook, I wrap the laces 3 times around each other, tie the bow, and then lock in each end with a half loop. (d) Not sure whether double lacing around the hooks is a good idea. May make it hard to adjust tension. You might want to get shorter laces and skip the double lacing along the hooks. Worth a try, and a lot simpler and quicker.


(4) Re-tie before you get on the ice. Boots and laces change shape just sitting around. Most figure skaters I see will lace up, skate around a while, and then leave the ice to re-lace. What I do: after I’ve laced up the first time, I do 20-30 deep knee bends to stretch out the boot and laces. I then unlace the portion around the hooks, snug up any slack along the eyelets, and re-lace along the hooks. I never have to re-lace during a session. One girl came up to me for help. She was constantly re-lacing, and she noticed that most skaters had to re-lace at least once after they went on the ice, but she saw that I never re-laced once I got on the ice. I gave her a demo and she was real happy.

(5) One issue that I'll mention, but doesn't apply to you. Make sure the laces are not twisted. You want them to lie flat against the tongue and boot surfaces. From your photo, I can see that you are a very neat and ti-dy person (just two minor twists)!

(6) On my old Riedell Royals, I used two short laces, instead of one long lace. That worked great, but I can't do that on my Jackson Elite boots because of the different eyelet and hook configuration.

Good luck!

After you lace the eyelets, do an over under to lock in the tension. Some do it double but my daughter just once.

Then on the hooks, for the last pair, she does a bow, hooks each loop over the the last hook and pulls the tails. Now the bow is no longer visible. Then she makes a second bow.

I think your laces are really long. That might be a difference in boot maker because riedell sends the boot with laces that don't have that excess.

Finally, make sure when you tighten laces you do it pulling the down, not up. It seems to be more snug without straining eyelets.
