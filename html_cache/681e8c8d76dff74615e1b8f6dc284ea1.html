This is a question about athletic strength as one ages. So it might be best answered by those who are in their senior years as I am.

First off, I am a quad roller-skater. I never took it up until I was 38. There aren't any competitive rinks where I live in West Virginia. (Indeed, even most of the recreational rinks have closed.) In a few years I got really good at the two-footed spin which I could do for a 50-60 seconds with my eyes blindfolded. (Never could do a one-footed spin. Guess I needed a coach.) I could skate down the length of the big rink with one skate at 90-degrees to my body over the 3-foot high rail. Now at age 70, I can't even get my leg up that high with street shoes.

I recall that back in the late-80s if I hadn't been skating for a couple of weeks and went out and did all those spins with varying arm movements all the time, my inguinal and groin muscles would be sore the next day. But if I resumed skating 2-3 times a week, that soreness would disappear.

My problem now is that I lose my balance trying to do one of those spins after only 5 seconds. I've also had an inguinal hernia operation. The doctor told me that I had weak muscles down there. But it was just due to ageing. He cautioned me about vigorous weight-lifting, however. He said that there is no known exercise that will make old inguinal & groin muscles as strong as they once were. And possibly I could make the hernia reoccur (or get a new one) if I pushed too hard.

I'm 5'10", 130-lbs, 70 yrs old. Just learned that I have osteoporosis to boot. I still do a lot of hiking and jogging in the woods. I ran a 4:30 mile when I was 17. But trying to sprint now causes my hamstrings to pull/strain.

Basically my question boils down to is there a time when I have to face reality and just admit that I can't do all the skating stunts (e.g., spins, jumps) that I once did? And that it isn't for lacking of trying but rather due to ageing and loss of muscle strength?

At my rink I see a lot of older skaters who once did spins and turns and but now do edge exercises and dance steps most mornings up and down the rink. They are very good and have gorgeous edges. You might find that trying to jump and spin is not wise now because if you fall and get injured, older bones are more brittle, take longer to heal and you could create a lot of problems for yourself.

But you can still SKATE! Personally, holding that glide and doing big patterns are what makes skating most exhilarating for me.

I was diagnosed with osteoporosis in my 40's (with it's precursor, osteopenia, in my 30's). I am 55 years old at present. It is true that the orthopedist thinks I would not have broken my wrist if I didn't have osteoporosis. The two spiral fractures caused just by a standing still fall, were what initiated the bone density test that showed I had osteopenia in my 30's. I have spinal degeneration also. But am I going to quit skating? NO! I get hurt more off of the ice than I do skating, and I do jumping and spinning. I plan on skating as long as I can stand up in my skates. When I retire from my full-time job, if I still live in my current area, the part-time job I have at the ice rink will be my retirement job. My orthopedist and my primary care doctor have nothing but encouragement for me to keep skating, so I will take their advice on the subject. Just check with your own doctors and see what they say.

I'm not qualified to answer this question by age, but I do want to share my two grains...


First of all, answering "Are Senior Skaters Limited?" the answer is plainly "Yes". You are limited, I am limited, a 5yr old is limited. In every sport we are limited by our bodies. And you're not going to have the same strength that you had with 20 years —but then at 13 you didn't either.
I think your question goes more on "what can I do with the limitations I have" and that's a really difficult question, because every one is different.

There are various senior skaters at my rink. I've never seen them jump (well, one of them has a waltz jump, but she doesn't do it really often and it's more of a step than a jump), but some do have good spins. And way better edges than I do.
And there are tons of videos on YouTube of people aged over 80 doing gymnastics, skating, etc etc. So it is possible .
I do know that with age injuries are more prone to happen and more difficult to happen, so please talk to your doctor before partaking any different activity.

And before closing this post. I've been painfully stretching for over two months now (I've never done any sports). I'm 21. I just got my splits on my good leg and I am really close on my bad leg. I (am) was super proud.
I've been away from home for those two months, yesterday I got back home. My mom was like: "oh, you got your splits, right? Show me!"
"I need to warm up fist! And I'm in jeans"
"Nah"
And she proceeded to do her full front splints, without warming up, on both legs, way better than I do, right there. She also did a vertical split. My jaw dropped to the floor (I then tried to do the same and got my right one, of course not my bad leg, but hers were way better).
She's 57. Ok, she was a professional gymnast, but she quit competitive training at 20 and quit all gymnastic activities at 25. So that's plenty of time between then and now.

Special case? for sure, I'm not going to be doing vertical splits. Those of us who haven't been trained before have it have it harder. But if you're careful with what you do and ask for professional advice I don't think you'll have a problem skating. Footwork for sure. Spins, probably. I'm not so sure about jumps. And always wear protective pads. Don't risk your health! And have fun.

I know at least one person in her 70s and several in their 60s who still do the same single jumps on ice that they've been doing for 20 or more years.

But they've been skating almost every morning for decades, with breaks of a month or a few months only for injuries or major life events.

I'm only in my 50s but I don't jump or spin any more because of hip issue that makes those moves painful and was probably caused or exacerbated by jumping. Some of my edge-based skills have also deteriorated, but others continue to improve.

I.e., everyone is different.

So if you love to skate, yes, check with your doctor, and then start out slow with an instructor who has experience teaching adults with different abilities, see what skills you can gradually work your way back to and maybe learn some new ones.

You may not be able to get back everything that you used to do. If you find the process too frustrating, you could choose not to continue.

Also, if you're switching from quad roller skates to ice, you'll want to take your time developing the different balance over blades than over wheels and learn other subtle differences in technique. Don't just jump in (so to speak) and expect everything to work the same way.

I would say yes- but honestly most all of us are limited in one way. I can stretch every day of my life and will never do a Beillman or hair cutter spin. But I can learn a good layback. My advice would be to find a coach you connect with and make step by step goals. Also- strength training! The thing that improves bone density is resistance training and weight bearing exercise (I am a physical therapist).

Thanks to all of you for giving me your thoughts and advice.

Perhaps I should have seen this coming a decade ago when I cut my grass for the first time in early Spring after being inside for Winter. I have to pull on the lawnmower (i.e., go backwards) in many places in my yard. The next day it felt like I ruptured myself. I had an ultrasound done and the results were negative. Their final analysis was a strained groin muscle. I couldn't figure that out. How did I strain it? Did I pull the lawnmower awkwardly in some spot? Now looking backwards, I think it was the first sign of age-related muscle deterioration.

Everything that is done in ice-skating can be done in roller-skating. I have all the testing and judging manuals. They even have roller-skating hockey and speed skating relays. I often wondered why it never became a summer Olympic sport. It has long had National and Pan American championships. I actually tried ice-skating for the first time several years ago. Since I was so good at roller-skating, I thought it would be a walk-in-the-park.. But I was wrong.

In roller-skating, your skates move from side-to-side. Going forward, most of the weight is on your back wheels (I'm talking quads here). Going backwards, nearly all the propulsive force is on your front wheels. I of course used that technique when I tried ice-skating. I couldn't move more than 10 feet at a time. There's obviously a different mode you have to use.

I've tried doing exercises like deep knee bends with and without a dumbbell. Decades ago when I did that (and that was rare; I never worked out; I guess I was just naturally strong in my legs) I never felt anything but boredom. Now my inguinal & groin muscles hurt after doing those. Especially the muscle where I developed the hernia. I even contacted a ballet and gymnastics class to see if I could get specialized instructions just for spinning and jumping. But they wanted me to enroll for their entire courses. I didn't want that. (I've often wondered why all the Olympic gymnastics champions are always so young. Why can't they replicate their skills 10 or 20 years later?)

For the record, I've only met one professional roller-skating coach in West Virginia. His name was Dale Johnson and that was 25 years ago. I don't know about ice-skating coaches. I've only seen one roller-skating rink that had figures painted on the floor. All others were "recreational" (i.e., not "competition"). I thought my 30-year old skates had gotten too heavy for me and that was the problem. I drove down to Kate's Skateland in Gastonia, NC. Billy Thompson customized a lighter pair for me leaving off the jump bar and not using the old rubber indoor/outdoor Kryptonics wheels that I skated with forever. But the new pair only helped marginally. I was still feeling inguinal muscle pain trying to stand up after "shooting the duck" backwards. (note: There are zillions of roller-skating wheels according to wheel width, axle diameter, "hardness", surface grooves, etc. Modern skaters choose based on the skating surface and their own preference. And it's not uncommon for experts to not use the same wheel on each axle. Now that's really splitting hairs!)

I used to subscribe to "SKATE" magazine. Now it is only printed digitally (i.e., no more hardcopy). On page 52 of the Fall/1986 issue is an advertisement for a "Jump Tender". A track is mounted on the ceiling of a rink and a support line descends from it. The line wraps around a skater as he/she practices double/triple jumps without fear of falling. Something like that might help me try to reclaim what was so very easy for me in the past. (You can see the "Jump Tender" by selecting the "Modern (Girls) Roller Derby" article at http://www.stealthskater.com/Sports.htm . Scroll down through the article until you find it.)

But I suspect that Age has done its damage and the muscles will never be the same. (Also maybe I did damage when I used to pick up kids and whirl them around while doing my spins. Maybe the muscles slowly deteriorated faster because of things like that.) It's just hard for me to accept . In my mind I know what to do. The spirit is willing but my body won't cooperate anymore. It's my pride and stubbornness (and watching teenagers doing half-as-good what I used to do) that prevents me from throwing in the towel. I wonder how retired professional basketball players live with themselves knowing that they can't dunk like they used to ...

I have no problem doing "normal" skating. There's no pain etc. It's the fancy stunts that are giving me trouble. I guess I didn't realize how much core strength you need to keep your balance doing those spins. Wearing quad skates, only the inner half of one wheel per skate actually touches the floor while spinning. My body must have reacting subconsciously when I would start to lose my balance by pulling my legs in closer or standing up straighter on one-half of 2 wheels. If you have strong muscles, you can probably get away with that without pain the next day. (A basketball analogy would be someone who could easily dunk at age 25 but now finds it difficult/impossible to do at age 70. Why? And what can be done to be able to accomplish that again?)

I actually talked to a sports doctor about lifting weights to try to strengthen those core muscles. He told me to forget it at my age. He said at worst, it might cause the old hernia to come back or to get a new one. Lighter weights are okay. But they won't restore my earlier strength. And that's what I want. I do yoga and martial arts stretches with no problem. But they don't restore strength.

I also talked to a plastic surgeon about tummy tucks. I thought they tightened abdominal muscles while removing excess skin doing those operations. But he said they don't tighten muscles per se. What is tightened is the "sheath" (for lack of a better word) that encloses/wraps-around the muscles. It gives the illusion of tighter muscles. But the reality is that the muscles underneath the sheath are still as loose as they were before.

I've done some research into ageing. Muscles and tendons get longer and less firm. That's why your feet get wider as you get older. You can't wear the same width shoes that you once did. Once I developed what was diagnosed as acid reflux. They prescribed Prilosec which turned out to be a disaster for me. It turned out that I was not producing too much stomach acid. (If anything, I wasn't producing enough.) The muscles around my esophagus valve had weakened allowing stomach contents to occasionally burp back up into my throat. ( A "barium swallow" test determined that.)

I was hoping to find a "magic bullet" pill/medication that would reverse the muscle deterioration. But apparently there is no such thing. I read something about an enzyme in males that is blamed for testosterone reduction. There is some medication that is available to stop/reverse that. But not without side effects. My mother always was irked by the phrase the "Golden Years". She wondered what-the-hell was so "golden" about them? I'm starting to share her sentiments.

I've archived some interesting medical-related articles at my Alt-Physics site => http://www.stealthskater.com/Medical.htm which talk about DNA repair, age-related healing, chromosome damage due to shorter telomeres, etc.

You might have something there. I didn't give all the details of the last couple of years since my hernia surgery.

I found that I was 10 pounds heavier when I got home after the surgery. I hadn't anything to eat which meant that they must have given 10 pounds of IV solution during the operation. Laparoscopic (sometimes called "keyhole") surgery involves making 1-inch incisions and then inflating the abdomen with carbon dioxide gas to let the surgeons do their thing. The main selling point is it is an outpatient surgery and it only produces a very small scar. (Of course, there are more severe types of hernias that would require a "conventional" surgery.)

Well, I retained this increased weight into my third month after the surgery. It caused me to gain 3-4 inches around my waist. None of my pants fit. I complained to the surgeon and they did all sorts of tests on me for seromas, IBS, etc. Everything came back negative. It's been 2 years now and I'm still 5 pounds over where I should be. Diuretics do not help in the least. The only thing that minimally helps is fasting. I can go days with no more than 500 calories. It doesn't seem to effect me any. I still hike up steep forest trails and jog through the woods.

I've noticed that every 2-3 weeks there is a 12-hour period when I'm urinating every 20-30 minutes. I did a Google search and it's called the "whoosh" effect. Women have them also after having operations like hysterectomies. And I'm really not drinking that much to produce it. Almost seems like fluid is stored somewhere. I read about fluid storage in interstial spaces. Lymphatic vessel damage can occur after an operation and it takes a lot of time for new vessels to be created.

The latest thing I came across was "soft tissue swelling". I know I don't have any fat on me (just loose skin). My hernia surgeon told me that it would likely take a "tincture of time" for me to get back to normal. (And maybe longer since you don't heal as quick as you did in your 20-30s.) My family doctor said that it may go away in time and it may never go away. A female friend (who is now 40) said that it took her 3 years to reduce her abdominal bloat after her hysterectomy.

But I'm digressing here. The main point is that there are many days when I'm on a very low calorie diet. It's the only way I can things in some sort of minimal control. I read Mayo Clinic medical reports that pill supplements don't do any good (and some like calcium pills can actually cause plaque buildup in arteries). Plus it doesn't seem that I can metabolize protein as quickly as I did 10 years ago. Now whether that is do to age or any intestinal rearrangement that occurred during the operation, I don't know. Before the operation I had to force myself to eat more to keep from losing weight. Now it seems I gain weight overnight without having eaten anything. (Go figure! And I've ruled out sleep-walking.)

But looking back, I was still having problems keeping my balance during those tricky spins BEFORE I had the surgery and went on the conservative diet. So even though a minimal protein intake probably isn't helping me any, the age-related muscle atrophy problem may still be there.
