My daughter has been skating since she was four. When she first started she showed tremendous talent and the coaches at the rink were all telling me to prepare for the long haul because she was going places. She wowed everybody. Fast-forward five years and we have a nine-year old who hasn't even started learning her axel. I think so much of it is because a) she has/had a coach who could only work with her when he had some time (sometimes once a week, sometimes once a month) and b) she would go months without skating because I didn't feel like taking her. But two months ago we got her an unlimited pass and a coach who can teach regularly and she went from her best moves being a waltz and a two foot spin to consistently landing all the singles except lutz (and axel obviously) and...well, I won't bore you with the details but she's a lot better. She loves it and she's happy but she feels like she's behind.

Anyway, I feel horrible, and anxious. I knew from the beginning that the odds of her making it to the big leagues were so small that I should just assume she wasn't really "going places," and that's even more true now, but I don't love being the parent of a skater with a lot of promise and passion but who can't do an axel after five years because I didn't have it together.

But ultimately this is not a big deal, right? I didn't blow any opportunities she may have had? I should just calm down and read a book or something?

You can't undo the past, so there's no sense getting upset about it. You can only decide what to do going forward. If she really loves it and you can afford it, how much time and money are you willing to devote to the sport?

You also don't know what would have happened if you had been a pushy tiger ice mom all those years. She may have advanced faster, but she may have also burned out or succumbed to injury.

I don't think it's useful to think in terms of "she's been skating for 5 years and she doesn't have an axel". It's more like she's dabbled in skating or skated recreationally for 5 years. It's unfair to compare her with someone who has seriously trained for 5 years.

OP, please don't feel bad! The fact that you have had involvement at all in your child's skating is wonderful. I'll be perfectly, bluntly honest: if they were right and she really had been going places (as in international elite competition), she probably isn't now. There's still certainly a chance. Johnny Weir started skating at 12. But it's unlikely. Johnny landed an Axel in a week.

That being said, you can't turn back time, showing her that you're upset will only make things worse, and there's a good chance that she wouldn't have made it anyway. The best you can do is support her now (but don't overcompensate by pushing her). Also, skating is very expensive, and even if she had had access to proper training, you might not have been able to afford it.

She's far from the only one who had a chance to make it big but didn't because of lack of opportunity/good coaching/etc. I might have had that chance, too, but I didn't live near a rink, and by the time I started, it was pretty much too late... or maybe it wasn't, and I'm just telling myself that so I don't regret things too much.

But you never know what can still happen (for your daughter, I mean). I like to think that if something is meant to be, it'll happen despite any setbacks. Maybe she was meant to be something else. I'm sure that was the case for me. Now that I'm settled into my career, I can get back to skating and train for whatever competitions I can handle now. Maybe I'll see your daughter at one someday! Good luck and God bless.

When coaches say a 4 year old or other really young beginner has talent like that - I’m assuming that they just observed she was really comfortable and moving around the ice- they typically don’t mean that they’re going to the Olympics or they could be an elite competitor or anything like that. Any responsible coach would not be able to tell at that age.

Having talent like that is important for becoming a really good skater - many people who do synchro, compete at regionals, or complete a senior test before age 18 - all of those are still on the table for your daughter, and being an Olympic skater was likely never a possibility. So, don’t feel bad, and it’s not a big deal!

Agreeing with the previous poster: If by "going places" the coaches meant Nationals and international competitions, that would have been overly optimistic even if your daughter had started training seriously at 4 or 5. There are more girls who have that potential than there are opportunities available at those levels. Some of them don't have the means to train as hard as necessary; some lose interest; some get injured; some just never get lucky at qualifying competitions and even if they're good enough to advance, they have a bad day or lose a close contest and end up as alternates. Chances are higher that your daughter would have been one of those talented skaters than that she would have made it to Nationals.

If by "going places" they meant going to qualifying competitions and passing all her tests, that's still possible. She's still only 9 and she has a good head start over skaters who first start skating at 9. If you have the time and the money to get her to lessons and practices several times a week now and can keep it up for several years, reaching middle competition levels within the age limits (in the US, juvenile before 13 and intermediate before 18) may very well be possible. How well she might do at those levels or whether she will want to continue to higher levels will depend not only on her physical talent and willingness to work hard, but also on the quality of the coaching she receives and the amount of lessons and practices and competition opportunities your schedule and budget can allow.

If you are able to provide the ice time and lessons with a good coach, and if your daughter is eager, by all means go for it. If serious training would put a burden on your family's resources, then don't think in terms of her skating as something to win at, but as skills to master and an activity to have fun with in practice and appropriate competitions and passing tests. Which are the same decisions you would have had to make any time in the past 5 years and may need to revisit any time in the next 5 or 10 years.

I suggest that you try to look at what you have done for her skating in a more positive light - you are the mother of a 9 year old who is quite accomplished in skating, landing all of the single jumps though loop and flip, from what you say. She started young and moved through the basic levels, and now she is just about ready to work on the Axel and soon doubles. She is only 9 and has already earned her identity as an accomplished skater, and is now at the age where she can skate and train with concentration, if that is what she wants to do - a lot of younger kids are not capable of that until 7 or 8 anyway - so she is not that far behind. Look at all she has accomplished in just two months of concentrated effort - from waltz jump to full-rotation single jumps - that is pretty good! - if you are able to keep giving her that amount of ice and coaching, a year from now she could have the Axel and a couple of doubles. I've seen videos of Sasha Cohen skating at age 8 or 9, and she didn't have much more than an Axel at that age, from what I recall. She absolutely could still compete in Juvenile by age 12 - or, if she missed that, she could still compete in Intermediate by age 15 - and after that there are no age limits. Try to help her not compare herself to others at her specific age, because she will make her mark and be satisfied by her beautiful and unique skating and performance, not by being the best skater of her age. When we see a beautiful performance that touches us, it can come from any skater, of any age and any skill level, as long as it is heartfelt and the skater gives us something unique and personal. That is why skating is a special sport that we all love. Aside from all that, if she loves skating, she has her whole life ahead of her to participate in it on her own terms. Even if she never makes it to nationals or competes internationally, 10 years from now she could have her gold tests on her college application or compete nationally, collegiately or even internationally as a synchronized skater; 20 years from now she could be a judge or a coach, or a sports psychologist or journalist. If she has a passion for skating, or whatever else she may discover (it could be that skating is not her true passion but a step towards finding it) there are no real age limits - the world changes and makes way for people who keep at it and follow their passion - doors open and the journey can lead places you never imagined. Skating gives many gifts beyond elite competitive success and can help your daughter grow into a confident, strong, persistent and cultured person - that is its true potential.

Not a fail, but a question of life priorities. My child trains in a competitive area with lots of freestyle skaters that place well in regionals, sectionals, some making it to nationals - and a small number of parents are a bit smug about how advanced their skater is. Confidence is important, but so is a bit of hubris. Why?

1) Skating plateaus - yes, there's a lot of double jumps at a young age going on, but then that's where it stays for a few years before any meaningful triple action happens. And some of the techniques being skated just aren't going to last in the long term, meaning undoing certain habits down the road. Not easy to do.

2) Injuries - the amount of injuries in skaters 9, 10, 11, 12-ish due to over-training is sad to see. Time off not doing jumps due to foot, knee, hip, back, etc. pain is real.

3) Thinking that a bad/missed competitive season won't happen to you. It does happen. Being able to have long-term goals that work for your skater really helps when a tough season is upon your skater.

So no, you have not necessarily missed the boat. Talent doesn't survive in this sport without patience and hard work. Short and long-term goals help keep the skater motivated (testing, competing, shows at the rink). The biggest thing to do is not quit at key ages.

Keep your options open and don't just put your eggs in a skating basket - having a life and interests beyond skating helps so much, too, when doubts creep in.

Good luck!

Yes, NBD.

1. She's still young enough that if she wants a shot at the big time, I think that can happen. Beyond just ice time, it depends on how much talent she has and how good her coach is.

2. Be realistic about what you're willing to commit before you get her hopes up too high. This sport requires a ton of support from family. It's not just expensive, it's also just inconvenient. There are not always great freestyle times or the ones that work for your schedule might be busy or don't work for your coach, etc. It's a challenge to make it all work. I know parents with kids at high levels who regret getting invested in this sport but are so invested that they don't feel that they can pull away now and break their child's heart, even though the child should probably just treat this as a more recreational thing. Think this through and then commit to a specific path - make some priorities and stick with them.

3. When you're thinking and talking about her axel, think in terms of hours on the ice not years. Also, remember that a good and consistent axel is very different from a bad and inconsistent axel. I know many kids who 'have an axel' but those axels vary dramatically. The kids who learn it quickly all seem to have more natural talent than the ones who take longer to learn it, but the quality of their axel seems related to their coach. Also remember that there's ice dance and other options for kids who don't learn their jumps very quickly or who have knee issues, etc. My point is, while each axel is a convenient yardstick for measuring progress and indeed it's a bottleneck where only about 10% of the skaters at the level below move beyond, it's not the end all and be all of figure skating.

It really depends.
Short answer is that yes there are 'diminishing returns.' More is not always better.

Longer answer is that for some skaters in certain situations it may make sense to have lots of lessons.

For instance, my skater can be a bit reckless at times (because he has so much fun skating and is so passionate about skating) and so I have always erred on the side of 'more lessons is better' because he's always safer in a lesson.

I also feel like for younger skaters, more is better for the same reason - it keeps them safer. Young skaters tend to forget the etiquette rules of freestyle sessions and put themselves in danger outside of lessons.

And also, speaking of the etiquette rules, one of the common rules is that skaters in a lesson get right of way over skaters not in a lesson (unless the one outside a lesson is running a program). And so for this reason, this allows a skater (particularly a shy or young skater) to get a bit more space on a busy freestyle session. For instance, my kiddo simply could not practice his Moves in the Field on a busy session without a lesson, it wasn't going to happen, someone would just cut him off or run into him. So as a parent I learned that my options to get him to practice MITF were to find the empty sessions (often during school hours) or put him in a lesson.

Obviously also it makes sense to have more lessons right before competition. And also when learning a new, difficult jump so that they aren't practicing incorrectly outside of lessons.
