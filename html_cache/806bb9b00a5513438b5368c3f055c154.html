Hi All,

My 11 yr old has her first competition coming up. It's a recreational competition (ISI) and she's doing a Drama routine, Compulsory and an Interpretive.

I'm not at all sure how she would prepare for the Interpretive competition (it's listening to music for a couple minutes - then making up a routine on the spot).

And, not sure what to expect in general! Any hints or tips would be great!
 
G

There isn't really a way to prepare for the Interpretive because she won't know in advance what kind of music she'll get.

It might be a good idea to practice in advance some easy steps that she can do to different rhythms (I find chasses are good for that purpose, two-foot slaloms with arm movements, maybe three turns if she's comfortable with them, etc.) and also some moves that she'd be comfortable doing to quick music (little hops? toe turns?) and some (e.g., spirals) that would work better with slow music.

And then be mentally prepared to listen to the music and see how it inspires her to move. It's not important to include jumps and spins in these events, but if the music sounds like it would fit well with her favorite jump or spin she can try it out the second time they play the music on the ice.

What a brave girl to do Interpretive in her first competition!

Not sure how ISI works that event but at our Showcase competition the competitors are called together and get to listen to the music 3 times, off ice. Then when they take the ice for their warm-up the announcer lets them know the music will start so they can try what's in their head out on the ice.
This is done twice at our competition. Then the skaters leave the ice and called for their turn.

As gkelly said, it truly is pot-luck as to what music is chosen. We actually had a Christmas tune at our recent comp.
Maybe you could do a little pretend practice at home. Pick some random music and see what she can think of on the spur-of-the-moment.

I always amazed at what the competitors come up with. Good luck to her!

I have my beginning skaters make a short list of things they would like to include in their interpretive program - typically a couple of jumps, a spin, and a couple of favorite moves. I tell them that as they listen to the music, they can decide what order to do those elements. We also divide the rink into areas, and try to do something in each area. For a first time, I usually just stick to three zones, as divided by the blue lines, but when the skater has a little more experience I might have them target each of the 5 hockey circles. Then we talk about how they might get from place to place - stroking, chasses, swizzles, whatever. Finally, we talk about expression and how the music might affect what we do - does the music make you want to skate smoothly, or march, or dance? If the music has words, is there anything that suggests a particular movement? Going in with a plan prevents the skater from freezing up or doing the same thing over and over.

I would ask her coach to help prepare her for the Interpretive. A coach can do things like:
- talk though possible types of music styles and some elements that may fit the style
- explain about using all the ice and not sticking to one spot
- practice with various cuts of music
- give some calming strategies to help if nerves set in
- show or direct you to video of past interpretive competitions

My skater would be so great at Interpretive but is unwilling to do it. Had one bad experience (the music was 'too girly') and he refuses to try again. But I know other skaters who just LOVE it. They like being free to do their own thing and just put on a show.

One of the things I love about it is that you can add it on to virtually any competition and thus increase the chances of earning medals without much additional prep. It's just a good gamble and the event is fun to watch too

Oooo interpretives are fun! It's all about trying to get the right character for the music, and then expressing it as much as possible. I agree that it's a fun exercise to practice with random styles of music (she doesn't have to actually do a program to it, it's just "Here's a tango. How would you act?" "Here's some rock and roll. How would you do that?" Maybe even watch an episode of So You Think You Can Dance and see how dancers tackle various styles.

Try to include one highlight which your daughter is good at, like a spiral or a split jump or a spin (although it's not a freeskate either so don't worry about elements - it's more about expression and having fun and going outside your comfort zone). Good luck to her!
