NO! First of all, with that foot shape Graf's are a no-go. Secondly, the Windsor is for Professional skaters, so it's more than overbooting. I know this isn't the news you wanted but it's what is needed. Overbooting and overblading is NEVER the answer no matter the situation.

Good luck in your skating journey!

Just a general comment, since posts similar to yours pop up now and then. Your rationale is based on the premise that advanced gear (boots or blades or both) have only pluses and no minuses (other than price) for skaters at any level. So, as long as you can afford it, you might as well buy the most advanced gear available. Well, that premise is false. For skaters who are not ready, advanced gear do have minuses; minuses that can hinder progress or cause injury.

As a rough analogy, a beginner driver would be better served by a Chevy sedan than a Ferrari Formula1 (even if they had the bucks).

* I'm not here to recommend a particular boot for you. But here are some comments concerning the problems with your current boots. I'm wearing the current Jackson 5362; and I wore a similar model that came before it.

* Jackson uses different grades of linings in their boots. Your Elles are a lower-end kit (boots with pre-mounted blades). On this forum there have been complaints that even in the Debuts (sold as separate boots only) the linings around the heel wear prematurely. In my two pairs of Jacksons, I've had no problems with the linings. When I went to get my blades sharpened a month ago, I compared the linings in my boots with those of the Debuts, and they are different. @Ic3Rabbit recommended that you consider the Synergy Elite. I didn't look inside one of those, but check with Jackson whether they use the same linings as the 5300 series boots. If so, the linings should be durable.

* You mentioned that there are no fitters close to you. So did you buy the Elles online? If so, I'm guessing that you did not have them heat molded. I checked; the Elles are heat moldable. Stock Elles come in two widths: R(A/B), W(C/D). A while ago, Jackson changed the widths of many of their boots: they use the same outsole to cover two widths. Thus, the R outsole is used for both A and B width; and the W outsole is used for both C and D width. For each outsole, the wider width is attained via heat molding/stretching of the uppers. If you didn't have this done already, that may be one simple fix.

* The toe box can be further rounded via punching and spot heating (done in multiple stages if needed). I've had this done to my boots, and was able to avoid the need for a Rapid Custom (see below). You need an experienced tech, and you need to leave the boots. This is a longer-lasting and more extensive procedure than quickie spot punching done cold.

* If heat molding/stretching/punching in a stock boot is not sufficient to give you sufficient width around the ball and toes, but the current heel fits snugly, then for higher-end boots, such as the Synergy Elite, you can order, via Rapid Custom, boots that are even wider about the ball, while keeping the same width heel.

* It's not clear to me that excess height in the toe box is indeed a problem. Right now you don't have enough width, your toes are squished, and some of your toes are being forced up. If you correct the width problem, this problem might go away. Also, it's likely that you will need to swap out the stock insole (footbed) for a different one to provide proper support for your high arches. The corrective insole will likely be thicker than the stock one, and a higher toe box will be advantageous to accommodate the extra thickness. That's a problem with boots with lower volume: they often don't have room for corrective insoles.

Not having a fitter nearby is a problem you'll need to work around. Especially as you progress to higher level (but still suitable for you) boots and blades, you'll typically need more than one visit to a tech. E.g., with boots, if you get an initial heat molding with the stock insoles, and then later get corrective insoles, you'll likely want to get a second heat molding with the corrective insoles. And with blades, you typically get an initial temp mount, try them out, get them adjusted, and try them out again. Often you'll need another iteration before the permanent mount. And you may need shims installed as well.

So you may need to plan an extended stay where you can skate several sessions near the fitter. Or ask around your local rink if there are coaches or other skaters who can handle some adjustments (such as blade mounting/alignment/shimming).

Unfortunately I'd be hesitant to recommend most of the lightweight blades currently on the market, for the reason that you've mentioned there aren't really any reliable techs or fitters in your area. The majority of lightweight blades available are more difficult to sharpen than regular blades, usually because of how they're constructed. MK/Wilson's Revolution blades, Ultima Matrix blades, and Paramount blades are all designed as a lightweight chassis (carbon fiber or aluminum) with steel runners attached - a design that usually requires the sharpening machine to have a special clamp for holding the skate during sharpening. The average skating rink pro shop is not likely to have the necessary equipment for sharpening blades like this. The Matrix blades and the higher-level Paramount blades are also stainless steel blades, which can be more difficult to sharpen than traditional carbon steel - and the more advanced MK/Wilson blades have features like tapering and side-honing that also make them more difficult to sharpen (this is true of Wilson's Gold Seal, as well as MK's Phantom and Gold Star).

One blade that comes to mind given your criteria is the Ultima Legacy 8. It's an intermediate blade, which would be appropriate for your level, and has an 8-foot main rocker, with a cross-cut toepick that I don't believe is particularly aggressive (I've seen a few pairs of the Legacy 7 blades, which have a pretty run-of-the-mill toepick, and I'd assume the pick on the 8 is similar, if not the same). It's even a lightweight blade - though rather than using the runner/chassis design, the weight reduction comes from several cut-out areas along the body of the blade, so it's much easier to sharpen. The catch is that many people feel the Ultima blades in general have flatter spin rockers than they would like - so you might find the Legacy 8 not as good for spins as you'd like (although since you're coming from the Ultima Mirage, it might still be an improvement - the Mirage is quite flat!).

I'm baffled. Why would you want to be able to put your hand down inside the boots? To figure skate properly and safely, you should have them so snugly laced all the way up that you *can't* get your hand in. To work the boots down to the level of comfort that you'd have from the start with the right stiffness level (which for you is not 90), you need to do a lot of power skating, and that uses deep knee bend and strong hip muscles, not "ankle strength". I hope you can get a chance to discuss your wants with a good fitter in Boston. Someone who knows what they're doing and can at least see your feet and how you walk, even if in this case there won't be an opportunity for the fitter to see you skate, is always better than trying to explain just in words.

Toe pointing, though, is much more a function of the skater's own turnout from the hip than the cut or stiffness of the boot they wear. Ice dancers do get a bit of advantage from a lower cut back, but not as much as you might think. Unlike in ballet where the feet are arched and the toes actually are pointed, in skating it's largely an optical illusion. The illusion is created when the skater's free leg is turned out from the hip, so that it's the front of the whole leg down to and along the lacing on the boot that is facing the boards -- what's seen by the spectators or the judges, if that's what you want. They don't see the outline of the heel and shaped sole of the boot, or the blade attached to the sole, "breaking" that line. If you haven't taken a barre class, that would be an excellent way to improve your turnout and present that illusion of the pointed toe.

I've never worn Graf or Edea, so I can't advise you there from personal experience. There's a mix on GS of fans, coaches, technicians, skaters. I'm a skater. I know what equipment I've used, and that of my several pairs partners over the years, and what I've heard discussed by friends in the dressing rooms in the couple of countries where I've trained and competed. If you can see a fitter in Boston, you'll get the advice of someone who knows a wider range of equipment. Good luck!
