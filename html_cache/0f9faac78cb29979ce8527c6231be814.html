Are you asking about Moves in the Field or Freestyle tests?

I've seen 5-year-olds pass Pre-Preliminary Moves, and I've seen adults pass it instead of or in addition to Adult Pre-Bronze.

With that first test, it mostly depends how old they were when they started skating, or started taking private lesson.

After that it depends not only on how old they already are but also on what their goals are. Do they want to compete, and in which discipline? E.g., I've seen synchro skaters, or a former ice dancer, working on junior or senior moves and pre-preliminary freestyle.

Here http://www.usfsa.org/content/AllAboutTests.pdf is a link to a USFSA note about tests and averages. They mention that for people skating regularly it takes between 6 months to 1 year to pass each MIF test. As far as age, I watch a lot of test sessions, and the youngest person I know who passed Senior MIF was 10 and the oldest person was 55 at the time they passed. If you want to get a sense of what's common in your area, you can go and watch some test sessions and it might be helpful to you as you think of testing as well.

At our rink, same thing, most skaters (if they don't quit early) test Senior FS by junior or senior year of high school before they leave for college. After high school, they may stop skating completely to focus on college and getting a real job. Or they may work part-time as a coach during college years or join a college synchro team etc.

Even some of our skaters who start as late as age 14 often "catch up" if they work really hard and are able to take their senior tests by Senior year of high school. Plenty of them though, have to skate during their college years to get to Senior level tests.

If they start at age 15 or later, it's really a crapshoot how far they will get because at that point, they're too old to catch up to what's "normal" for the kids who started young. But I think anyone in great shape and younger than age 40-50 has a good shot at making it all the way to Senior eventually, as long as they can dedicate enough time to skating and aren't too fearful.
Someone over age 50 might be able to do it, but it depends on how "fragile" their body is at that point and if they can take the falls from failing jumps.


In general, at my rink:
pre-pre to prelim is mainly 7 to 10 years old
pre-juve to juve is mainly 10 to 14 years old
intermediate to novice is mainly 14 to 17
junior-senior is very rare at my rink because most skaters quit before then, or stay at the intermediate/novice level for competitions and never complete their tests. Mainly tho, it is 17-18 years old taking these tests. Occasionally, you'll see someone in their early 20s or 30s taking these tests to "finish" what they started as a kid long ago, or because they started late.

In my mind,
pre-pre to prelim are the "little kids".
Pre-juve to Juve are the "pre-teens".
Intermediate to Novice are the "teenagers".
Junior-Senior are "special" and "rare" and usually late teens or young adults.


We are a small rink and therefore not one of those highly competitive places. In a highly competitive rink where triples are common, these ages would be much younger.

There's not really 'average'. There are basically these kinds of skaters:
- Elite
- Recreational child/teen
- Adult (means recreational adult, not elite)
- Adaptive (Special Olympics etc)


Here are some guidelines for the elite group:

“Does your skater have what it takes to be elite? Figure skaters who fit the profile of potential to become elite national, international, and world-level athletes:

“Ages 6-8
Well-executed single jumps
Strong expressed desire to learn
Skating 5-6 days weekly, participating in off-ice programs such as conditioning, dance, jump classes, etc.

“Ages 9-11
Immersed in skating, all doubles landed with strong foundation on double axel and early triples

“Ages 12-16
Double axel, triples and triple triples, potential to do triple axel and quads

“Ages 17-20
Men: Triple axel and working on quads
Women: Full set of triples”

source: https://grassrootstochampions.com/ya...c-elite/#chart
Click to expand...
and

For skaters who start young AND have the goal of international competition, SkateCanada suggests:
Girls ages 3-8 do the learn to skate program and only do 1 competition per year.
Girls ages 7-11 should skate 2-5 days a week for 45-60 minute sessions each time and do 2-3 competitions per year.
Girls ages 9-13 should skate twice a day 4 or 5 days a week and do 6 competitions per year.
Girls ages 10-16 should skate 2 or 3 times a day 5 days a week and do up to 15 competitions per year.
Girls ages 13-19 do 3-4 sessions per day 5 days a week and up to 17 competitions per year.
Adult women should skate 1-3 times per day 1-6 times per week depending on goals (some are still competing/doing shows and some are transitioning to coaching etc)

Boys ages 3-9 do the learn to skate program and only do 1 competition per year.
Boys ages 8-12 should skate 2-5 days a week for 45-60 minute sessions each time and do 2-3 competitions per year.
Boys ages 10-14 should skate twice a day 4 or 5 days a week and do 6 competitions per year.
Boys ages 11-17 should skate 2 or 3 times a day 5 days a week and do up to 15 competitions per year.
Boys ages 14-21 do 3-4 sessions per day 5 days a week and up to 17 competitions per year.
Adult men should skate 1-3 times per day 1-6 times per week depending on goals (some are still competing/doing shows and some are transitioning to coaching etc)

source: https://skatecanada.ca/wp-content/uploads/2014/09/Skate-Canada-LTAD-Model-EN.pdf
