Look, I totally get where you're coming from with the money situation. I had a rough couple of months while I was at uni where I had to cancel several lessons because I just didn't have the money to pay my coach. Skating on a low income does tend to suck sometimes.

With all that said, there is a massive difference between three lessons a week and one lesson a month! Surely there's some middle ground there. I take one half-hour private lesson a week. It's all I've ever taken (except on very rare occasions where I've had the opportunity/money/energy for a second lesson in a week). I've still made plenty of progress - I was reflecting on that tonight during my lesson, actually, as my coach made me work the first part of my program again and again, thinking that a few years ago, I would not have been able to do the transitions I was doing into the jumps (I'm especially fond of my Salchow and loop entrances this year). So there are gains to be made on less time.

It should also be pointed out that lessons are nothing without practice. No point having three lessons a week if you're not practicing outside them as well.

I think it would be worth it for a lesson every month if that is all you can do. I also think 3 lessons a week would almost be overkill for someone just starting out.

If you do take a lesson a month I would have your coach focus only on one or two skills in the lesson. That way when when you practice them you will be sure to do them correctly.

Skating takes a long time to learn. Progress is measured month to month not day to day. If you learned something as "simple" as crossovers from your coach in your monthly lesson and then practiced only crossovers till your next lesson in a month you would not be wasting your time.

Coebalt said:
I guess I don't really know. I figured that if I took lessons multiple times a week, in addition to skating whenever possible, that maybe I could get a jump or two before the end of the year.
Click to expand...

That's a reasonable expectation. Waltz jump as soon as you're comfortable with back crossovers, back outside edges, and forward three turns.

So start working up to those skills now, by taking in-person lessons less often and practicing what you learn.

Salchow and toe loop after you're comfortable with waltz jumps.

I know I'd like to at least /start/ working on an axle within a year and a half or two(not having it down consistently, just you know, working on it), for personal reasons.
Click to expand...

To start working on the axel, you would want to have a solid backspin and waltz-loop combination.

That might be possible with the 3 lessons a week plus several more hours per week of practice for 1.5 to 2 years. Especially if you're a child or young teenager.

If you're a full-grown adult, if and when you get to the point of trying to learn an axel, you're probably going to get stuck there for years if not forever. Very very few skaters who start as adults ever learn axels. Many adults get stuck on the loop jump.

(And many kids get stuck on the axel, or sooner. And then they quit. Or switch to ice dance or synchro.)

You won't know what your potential is until you start skating, start taking lessons, and start progressing. And then see what comes easily to you and what's more challenging for you.

The sooner (younger) you start, the better, even as an adult. If you can already do a salchow before you start multiple lessons a week, you'll be able to progress more quickly at that point than if you're still working on one-foot glides. Get the lessons on the one-foot glides now -- don't wait until you can afford the multiple lessons.

How much you improve is tied to how much you practice in a correct manner. Remember, practice makes permanent. Don't practice wrong things. A coach's purpose is to teach you correct technique.

There is a balance between coaching time and practice time. If you're taking so many lessons that you don't have your own practice time without the coach watching you, inevitably you'll be paying the coach to be watching you practice. This is not an efficient use of your money. This may also be annoying to the coach, especially if the coach has ambitious expectations for his/her students.

Take lessons, but also be an independent learner. Work with a coach just enough to know what to practice correctly, and then practice on your own, paying attention to what your coach teaches you. A previous poster mentioned a ballpark of 2-3 hours of practice per hour of lesson. From my own experience, that seems to be about the right balance.
