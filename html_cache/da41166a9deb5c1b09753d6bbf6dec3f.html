Again, you have to define what you mean by "professional" or "the top" -- what your goals are.

If you mean competing at Nationals or internationally, don't count on it. First of all, almost no skaters can reach that level no matter how young they start. Even if you were 5 years old and lived near a rink with lots of ice time and good coaching and you had lots of money to pay for it, the odds would be against reaching an elite competition level.

It's that much less likely if you start later, or if several hours of lessons each week and several hours of practice almost every day will not be possible.

Realize that the amount of lesson and practice time that it takes not just to reach the higher test levels but to be competitive at those levels costs a lot more money than most families can afford.

Even skaters who do start that young and do have all those advantages might get hampered by injury or might find that they don't have the right body type for the more difficult moves.

If you can start with weekly lessons and several days of practice, and if it will be possible to increase that time as you progress, and if you find you have some aptitude for gliding and edge control, skating fast, and also power to jump high and coordination to rotate in jumps and spins, you might be able to reach a middle test level, maybe even to pass senior tests if you work hard and often for enough years. Your dance background would be helpful.

If you're interested in competing, in the US you can do that at beginner levels and at each test level as you move up: pre-preliminary, preliminary, pre-juvenile.

You're already too old to compete at juvenile level, and your chances of passing the intermediate tests by the time you're 17 are slim. If you can pass your juvenile tests in a few years, you could compete at Open Juvenile level -- jumpwise the test requires a single axel, and competitions allow any doubles.

Novice tests are a step up from there in difficulty, but if you can get to that level there is no age limit to compete in the US, so that would be your best bet for ever competing at Regionals. It's unlikely that you'll get there, but it is within the realm of possibility, so you could start out with that as a long-term goal.

The range of skill levels is wide at those competitions -- some skaters just barely passed their tests and can just barely land a few double jumps -- a few of the best intermediates and more of the novices, especially the boys, can land several triples as well as skating with speed and polish. If you are able to approach those levels in a few years, you would see where you fit in. Spending time at rinks as you take lessons and practice, and going to test sessions and competitions when you're ready to start testing and competing, would give you a good sense of what the skill levels are, how many recreational or average competitive skaters there are, and how few really good ones, and where you can fit in.

Meanwhile, enjoy the process of learning and set some reasonable short-term goals for yourself -- passing tests, learning new skills, performing programs, maybe exploring synchronized skating or artistic skating or solo ice dance as well as singles skating.

Competitive skating for the most part is a hobby, not a profession, even for most elite competitors. It's an expensive sport, not so much at the lower levels, but more and more the higher up one gets. Only the very very best international-level skaters can earn more in prize money or get paid more to perform in shows than they spend on training, travel, etc.

If by "professional" you mean get paid to coach skating or to skate in shows, that might be possible for you, but you would have to reach a relatively high skill level first, and even so the pay (especially for performing) may not pay back the expenses of getting there.

Take lessons, practice, pay attention to the opportunities at your rink and elsewhere, have fun, and see how far you can go.

Just for some perspective, here was my experience at about your age:

My sister and I started lessons when I was 10 1/2. I liked it, so I kept going after my sister had gotten the basics of skating forward and then quit. But I was only skating about 2 hours a week. After 2 years, I could do a sitspin and a salchow and only the basic turns I needed to set up my easy jumps and spins.

At almost 13 I got more serious -- I started skating at least 10 hours a week and taking lessons from a more serious coach and practicing school figures, which were very demanding exercises skated around and around on circles on a small patch of ice and which were the basis of most testing at the time.

After another year and a half, I had passed my preliminary figure test and could land all the single jumps including single axel, and I was working on double salchow and more difficult spins.

But in those days, almost all of skating was geared toward competing at Regionals, and beyond if you did well enough. Most of the skaters I practiced with who were my age had passed the tests to compete in intermediate or novice, and some of the younger ones competed in juvenile or were on track to get there before they were 13. But very few of them were good enough to move on past Regionals.

There were few club competitions in those days, and those that existed offered lower level events only for kids who were still young enough to be aiming for juvenile level. If you were already a teenager and not yet good enough for intermediate, there was no competition open to you. And our club skating show was only for skaters at competitive levels -- they didn't even have group numbers for those of us who weren't there yet.

For a while I kept working in hopes that I could get to intermediate when I was 17 and maybe novice when I was 18, but I knew it was a longshot. It's too bad there was no Open Juvenile option in those days, because that would have been a good goal I could have reached during my high school years. So eventually I quit -- able to land an axel, but never having skated a program. And my parents were just as happy not to wake up early and drive me to the rink and spend so much money on a hobby.

When I came back to skating as an adult, I was happy to get the chance to finally skate programs and enter competitions, but I was sorry I hadn't stuck with it a few years longer as a kid and gotten more skills when I was younger.

For the most part, the younger you learn skating skills, the more comfortable they feel to you and the more ease you can perform them with. That's one reason why almost all top competitors are skaters who started at young ages.

You're luckier that I was, because there are a lot more opportunities available to you in today's skating world. There are many competitions at that will welcome you before you pass tests and at each level as you move up. There are different kinds of skating you can focus on, different kinds of competitions you can enter if intense freestyle turns out not to be your favorite. Moves in the Field tests are more fun to practice and easier to pass than the school figures they replaced. Your rink or your skating club when you're ready to join one might host shows that offer opportunities for skaters of all levels to perform.

So I encourage you to get involved and take advantage of the opportunities available to you to develop skating skills and measure your progress and put it all together in programs to show what you can do, and have fun and achievable goals along the way.

I started late, and once when I was around 16yo, I heard my coach tell some lady that I was a natural skater, but started too late to get anywhere. When you are out of college and settled down in your life and have free time that college took away, you would hopefully remember how much you loved skating in your youth and return to it as an adult skater, as many people do.

Yes, any previous athletic or dance experience would be helpful.

But some sports are easier to reach elite levels in at all (because there are more opportunities at the top and/or fewer athletes trying to get there), and some are more forgiving to later starts than others because they don't rely so much on skills learned when the body is small and the neural pathways still developing.

You have to pass each test (Moves in the Field plus either freestyle/pairs or pattern and free dances as applicable) before you can take the next test. But you don't have to compete at every level. If you can pass five levels in one or two years, you can go straight to intermediate competition. But you would be competing against other skaters with a lot more experience.


I once knew a woman who had an Olympic medal in a sport that is less popular than skating (competitively anyway -- people do it recreationally) and less dependent on specific skills and aptitudes developed from a young age.

Her daughter, then about 9 or 10, was interested in figure skating, so she asked my advice.

The first thing I said was that she was never going to the Olympics, so not to make that a goal.

Possible paths would be
1) a recreational approach, skating just a couple hours a week, maybe not every week, master single jumps and maybe learn some beginning doubles, and compete for fun if she liked
2) a serious approach to training and competing, probably get to intermediate level in about 5 years, and then see what happens
3) whether elite skating, with double axels and triple jumps, would be possible would become clearer once she got to the point where the next step would be learning double axel, and whether it's possible depends more on natural talent and body type than on desire and hard work, although those (and good coaching, and lots of practice time) are necessary too. I said "You don't choose elite skating, it chooses you."

They chose 2). The skater did end up competing at intermediate level and attempting double axels. That takes above-average talent and dedication and put her well in the upper half of all competitive skaters, but not the tiny percentage on track to reach national much less international competition. Then in her later teens she chose to focus more on academics and "tested out" -- passing all her tests but not competing at the higher levels.

Because you have to be under 18 to compete at intermediate level, it would be a harder goal to achieve for someone who starts a few years later, but it could be possible.

Try going to youtube and searching for "Open Juvenile freeskate," "Intermediate freeskate," and "Novice freeskate" to get a sense of the skill levels I'm talking about. Ignore any examples from Nationals -- those are exceptional skaters. But look at performances from nonqualifying or regional competitions (serious or semi-serious competitors) or test sessions (some might be just recreational/test skaters).

That could give you a sense what might be possible with several years of training starting as a young teen.

I have brought something you happened to say in your other thread about synthetic ice over here, b/c my reply is unrelated to synthetic ice.

As you try to solve your problem re access to (real) ice, have you considered the possibility of skating before school?

Yes, it would mean waking very early in the morning; persuading a supportive parent (who presumably has a full-time job) to drive you round trip very early in the morning; etc.; etc.
All easier said than done.
But it is not unheard of for other serious young skaters and their families to make great sacrifices in sleep/time/convenience (as well as money, of course) in order for the skater to skate.

One example:
In Tessa and Scott's book, they wrote about being young kids (younger than you) and waking very early in the morning several times a week so that a parent could drive them over an hour (each way) from the London/Ilderton area to a desirable coach at the Kitchener-Waterloo club. After their early-morning session, the parent would drive them back to London/Ilderton for their regular school day.
(Tessa and Scott carpooled, so at least their parents could share the driving burden -- but you get the point.)

Another example:
I highly recommend that you watch this short video from Adam Rippon: http://www.wegetup.com/skaters/adam-rippon/
It's adorable, and it's inspiring. At age eleven, Adam set his daily alarm for 5:00 am. He would wake his mother up, and make bacon and coffee for her to take in the car when she drove him to the rink. Sometimes the guy at the rink was late, so Kelly Rippon ended up getting a key to the rink and learning to drive the Zamboni.
Adam is the oldest of six children, so it's not as if his mother did not have younger kids to care for at the same time. Nothing about this crazy schedule/routine could have been easy. But somehow they managed. (Other bits that I have read from/about his mother elsewhere also give me the impression that she is an amazing person .)
