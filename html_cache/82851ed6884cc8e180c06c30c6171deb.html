Hi,
I'm an adult and have been skating for four years (well, maybe like two and a half, considering the rink closures).
What really helped me was plyometrics. My coach gave me exercises, but there are plenty on YouTube. Just getting a bit more muscle on my thighs and getting the feeling of actually jumping instead of hopping and hoping for the best.
I am also a yogi (even before skating) and I think that helps a ton for core and balance and flexibility. You don't need to stretch for 45 min a day, just fifteen is enough if you are consistent with it. It is a question of diminishing gains.
I do not find the spinner very useful, it is quite dangerous to use it if you don't have proper flooring to actually use it. It will slip on tile floor and scratch wooden floors. So I have not done that a lot.
Maybe ask your coach to put together a workout?

You don't need to stretch for 45 minutes. Just about any type of cross training exercise is good to help prevent injury. At a more beginner level though, on ice practice is what will lead to you seeing the most results, especially working on building strong basic skating skills. Edgework can actually help you improve quite a bit on jumps and spins. I'd recommend also looking into doing some beginner ice dance pattern dances if you can.

The thing that really helps progress as a beginner is more regular skating. For example, if you can only skate for two hours a week, try to do it in two sessions of one hour. Skating twice a week makes a huge difference to your muscle memory.

Stretching for 45 minutes a day? Way too long. Definitely some stretching but anything after 15 minutes isn't really going to help much. (Personally, I'd just go to sleep on the mat!)

You can do all manner of off-ice classes online and by Zoom these days, and they are good to do at all levels, but at this stage what really helps is actual skating.

Just an update here. I had a toe + foot injury which cut me out of 1/2 of the winter season. During that time, I did core and arm workouts in addition to pilates and floor barre. I would say the core and arms made the biggest difference. I was able to effortlessly check and had better balance when I finally got back on the ice. My coach said I got better during the time off and encouraged me to take more breaks. So there you have it!

I thought I had to focus on stretching, but my coach said I don't need to do much of that because of my hyper mobility. I keep getting foot injuries, so I stopped jumping last September and was working on building ankle and foot strength with ballet. I had to stop taking regular ballet classes because they aggravate my foot and now I just do floor ballet or similar non-weight bearing classes. Those tend to have a ton of sit ups in them, so they are helping.

Thanks for all your tips!

When I took up skating in my late 20s, my schedule in grad school was horrendous, and I didn't have ice time when I'd get off of a 24h call. What I did when I wasn't on the ice was go to a concrete basketball court or wherever and do my ice patterns but with roller blades. Back crossovers, spirals, even jumps, etc. And my roller blades were HEAVY. I saw immediate results on ice when I put my skates on and just went out there on the ice and freestyled. Kind of like how Ty Cobb would wear weights on his ankles during the day so that when he played games his feet felt light as a feather. You can spend hours in a dance studio, but as a beginner you want to build the muscles quickly. I'd say go rollerblade in a rink and pretend you are wearing ice skates.

Ic3Rabbit is right, core is important.

Specifically in your case you mentioned hypermobility in ankles and knees...I don't see your body so I can't make a rehabilitation check-up. But hypermobility can be in other joints as well, just you didn't put attention to it because it didn't bother you. You definitely need to do exercises to stabilize hypermobile joints. You also mentioned weak upper body - which may lead to bigger kyphosis of thoracic spine, bigger lordosis of lumbar spine, pelvic anteversion... In this case you really need to be careful and control each exercise to be done rightly. You will need second person to control right posture in exercises (mirror is partially helpful only).

I would advice not to forget about:
1) deep stabilizing muscle system - diaphragm, musculus transversus abdominis, multifidi muscles, pelvic floor muscles
2) right position of body segments.

Social nets are full of videos with people demonstrating different kind of exercises, some of those people are gym instructors. Frankly I am negatively surprised how many of them are IGNORING THE NEED OF RIGHT EXECUTION. And it leads to health troubles not only for them but also for people who follow them. Which only supports my idea that even workout in a gym should be controlled (even if through video only) and corrected by people working in rehabilitation industry (physiotherapist or doctor) to avoid health issues.

For example if you lay on the back on the floor and lift your legs - with wrong activation of deep muscle system – if you see that with lifting legs lumbar part of back goes into lordosis and pelvis into anteversion = THIS IS WRONG - instead of working on your abdominal muscles you overwork lumbar part of back and destroy lumbar vertebral discs. Such wrongly done exercises are dangerous for your health especially if you repeat them many times per day for many years. Those exercises are also much less effective – in case mentioned above you don’t get maximal strenght from abdominal muscles and you learn wrong motoric stereotypes which become automatic and fixed with time.

Many years ago I had a possibility to take part in a lecture of professor Pavel Kolar, out great physiotherapist who works with top athletes (many of them are Olympic and World champions) from different kind of sports from many countries. He was presenting examples of top athletes with postural dysfunction (mainly examples from tennis, hockey, weight lifting). He didn’t show an examples from figure skating, but the principles are the same. Top athletes are ideal for presenting postural dysfunctions. Thanks to almost no fat their postural dysfunction and wrong muscle activation are well seen.

I loved Mr. Kolar’s lecture. It helped me to understand how much important deep muscle stabilizing system is, to realise how many things right body posture may influence. Unfortunately it also led me to realise how much ignored those things are in figure skating both off and on the ice in our country (well, in other countries as well looking at body posture of some skaters).

People (no matter if athletes or not) with postural dysfunction have typical body posture and muscle imbalances – for example one of signs can be very visible and hypertonic lumbar paravertebral erectors (from overworking mentioned above), pelvic anteversion. Postural dysfunction increases the speed of degeneration = artrosis of joints, in case of knee menisci are dammaged like the first. Postural dysfunction sooner or later always lead to serious health troubles and surgeries.

If your deep stabilizing muscle system is rightly activated, it means your body must be in the right position.
If you don't have right position of body segments, your deep muscle system cannot be rightly activated - it is a rule.

Last September I made a video for an athlete to demonstrate how good control of body segment's position and centering joints including look like. I used Tessa Virtue & Scott Moir's off ice exercises. Here is an excerpt from it.
(Please, don't use jumping for change of position when you have hypermobile joints).
