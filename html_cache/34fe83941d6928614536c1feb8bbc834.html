Skaters Edge said they didn't stock GRAF anymore so couldn't order them for me, as they had long wait times for restocking and no one asked for them. They do have Jacksons (I asked specifically about Supreme Pro 5320) and Risports in stock.

===

I saw my podiatrist who seemed to understand the different mechanics of skating as he mentioned edges. When thinking about the heel lift, ruled out usual issues causing that in walking shoes and thought it must be the skate fit, as the foot is so locked in the boot. He said my foot has a natural low arch, which you can't 'fix' and isn't a problem, you just support it when under load or causing problems. Coincidentally I met up with my dad who I noticed had the same arch and foot shape. Go figure

He attached a C-shaped semi compressed felt arch support under the Jackson stock insole, saying it was easier to save boot space this way than a custom orthotic. He gave me a cutout of a thinner thickness C shape cutout felt to use if this one felt too much support, or I could add it to this one if I needed more support.

He also gave me some felt circle pads of the same felt material to put over a metatarsal bone spur and wondered if part of my forefoot numbness was actually due to compression of the bone spur and referred nerve numbness, not necessarily forefoot compression.
He advised me too to skip that bone spur area when tying, so I skipped two holes up around that area and tied the whole foot less tightly.
(For anyone reading, he said the adhesive semi compressible felt material is easy to buy on aliexpress/temu or locally, and it seems of a good foam density to me - easy to experiment with)

===
Outcome:

The arch support made quite a big difference to my feeling of ankle support and it felt like my ankle was locked in for the first time after tying it tightly, and I was skating through my leg rather than my foot, if it makes any sense. I noticed too that my blades were more balanced at rest (or even slightly tending to an outside edge), previously they were always on an inside edge standing still. I still find outside edges hard but I think that may be less due to my foot problems and more due to technique, as it doesn't have as much of the 'collapsing' feeling as it did before.

My feet didn't go numb, possibly due to the circle pad or and/or the toe box stretching by the shoe repair shop. I wonder as well, that maybe I can tie my boots more effectively now, as before tying them tighter in any area could tend to push my weak arch down and cause issues.

The heel lift has gotten a bit better with the better ankle fit + a large heel grip, I can rock onto my forefoot without excess heel lifting. If I take the heel grip out my heel does still raise however.
The podiatrist said the heel grip is important to be in the right place but is quite hard to place. For me it was about 1-2cm above the bottom. As you want the stick out part to be above the curve of your heel to stop it lifting, not at the bottom of the shoe.

I'll go to Auckland (well my flights are non-refundable anyway!). I want to get the best fit, and even with less pain and fit issues, I wonder if the heel could fit better and you've emphasized the importance of the heel.
I'm curious too that the skate tech said from my virtual fit measurements that I would fit a Jackson C rather than D, it is strange. Perhaps they are talking about the heel.
I imagine the heel grip isn't a permanent solution, that it might easily break down compared to a proper boot material.
I'm more hopeful I'll be able to either make Jacksons work with some modifications. So I can use these boots for now for classes, and I can find another model that works like the Jackson Supreme 5320, perhaps in a split width for the heel fit.

I don't have medical training. I can't speak to special needs other than my own. E.g., my feet collapse under weight symmetrically - so I haven't tried to compensate for asymmetric internal foot support. And I don't have high or low arches.

Obviously, skate outsoles have to be extremely rigid, to avoid warping and breaking blades. (Interestingly, I just looked at my cross country skate boots. They all have extremely rigid outsoles too, though I'm not sure that is true of all XC ski boots.) But I use currently foam insoles for me, which are not rigid, and which do eventually compact down. Perhaps using a more stable foam (in the long term) like urethane, or using leather, would substantially increase the lifetime of my insoles.

I personally had bad experiences with completely rigid (but heat moldable) thin insoles (I think they were Superfeet brand, and were specifically designed for ice skates). First, because they rocked all over the place inside the boots. Second, because the rigid insole gradually stretched my boots outwards, so the bottoms fit loosely. But maybe similar heat moldable orthotics created by medical practitioners fit the boot better, so the latter problem need not occur?

I think most medical foot practitioners (like most podiatrists) concentrate on ordinary people, and for athletes, on ordinary athletic shoes. The height of the boot above the ice, and the sideways pushing, creates a lot of extra torque, compared to ordinary walking and many sports. Plus, proper skating technique requires a lot more deliberate pronation and supination (because we skate on and transition between edges more) than ordinary walking and many sports. And figure skaters in particular, are sometimes expected to skate along a constant arc radius, which requires holding a specific pronated or supinated position for an extended time. Figure skating jumps are also somewhat different than some sports, in that we are expected to rotate (in some cases, very fast) during most jumps, and the aesthetic rotational jump standards of figure skating (e.g., that the foot shouldn't rotate during the take-off and landing, which I'm told differs from the balletic standard) are quite artificial.

So you can't expect all medical practitioners to be experts on the needs of ice skaters. Which is why, if you see a medical practitioner, I think you should look for one who other skaters, especially figure skaters, have had good results from. E.g., there is a podiatrist around Baltimore, MD (USA) who many skaters use. I've met many figure skaters who rave about him, and many skate techs refer customers to him, so he has lots of experience with skaters. He also played hockey, and his daughter competed at the highest figure skating levels I know of another podiatrist in Delaware (USA) with a great reputation among skaters and skate techs too. It wouldn't surprise me if there are very few podiatrists and orthotists in the world who have similar reputations and level of experience with figure skaters. You might not find someone that world class in your area, but I wouldn't assume that someone who is very good at treating non-skating athletes will be equally good at treating skaters. Skaters, like performance dancers, are very much a specialty. Maybe figure skaters are even more so.

You need both a competent Jackson authorized fitter and a competent Jackson service agent. Jackson was bought up by a large sports conglomerate about 4 yrs ago, and I don't know how they do business now. But previously, prior to the introduction of the Jackson scanner, or if the authorized fitter did not have the Jackson scanner, the fitter took two sets (one while the skater was sitting and one while the skater was standing) of detailed measurements (including heel measurements) and foot tracings and sent the measurements and tracings to Jackson. Jackson supplied standard forms specifying how the measurements and tracings were to be performed. A Jackson tech would review the info and make a recommendation for proper fit.

See if Jackson still supports this process, and then try to find the closest authorized fitter who has experience with it. It's absurd for anyone to tell you to fit yourself.

Remember, the more advanced Jackson stock boots come in split widths, with the heel width nominally one size less than the ball width. When Jacksons were supplied in a single ball width, this was clear: A (ball)/AA (heel); B (ball)/A (heel); C(ball)/B (heel); .... With the current dual-ball-width sizing, the situation is not as clear. The two stock ball widths are R(A/B) and W(C/D), as I explained in a previous post. I emailed Jackson to get clarification on what the corresponding heel sizes are. They first gave me outdated info. After several rounds, they told me: R(A/B) [ball]/AA [heel] and W(C/D) [ball]/B [heel]. I don't have complete confidence in this answer, but that's the best info I got.

FWIW, I have an older pair of men's Jacksons when they were sized in single-ball width (nominally D ball/C heel) and a newer pair of men's Jacksons sized in dual-ball width [nominally (C/D) ball/B heel]. The heels feel the same. Too many variables to make a conclusion as to why that is.

If your ball width is truly greater than a D and your heel width is truly less than an A, then it's highly unlikely you will fit any stock boot from any manufacturer.

I've never ordered customs, but here are three tidbits.

* One skater I know ordered Jackson customs. The heels had a bit of slip. Her tech wanted to send them back for modification. But she didn't want to wait any longer, so accepted them; that was her call.

* In the US, a company well-known for customs is Harlicks. My coach has been a Harlicks fan since she was a competitive and show skater decades ago; she recommends them for her skaters who need customs. But even they sometimes screw up. One year, three skaters got their boots about the same time (from the same local competent tech). Their boots didn't fit right and were sent back. Two were satisfactorily modified. The third was too far off and would have had to be redone from scratch. Since the turnaround at the time was about 1 yr, the skater opted for a refund.

* My current tech told me he was once contacted by Jackson because he had the lowest rate of returns for customs, and they wanted to go over his procedures. Turns out there's a lot of art in taking manual measurements and taking manual tracings.

* So, again, confirm with both Jackson and your dealer what their current policy is for customs that don't fit. Make sure the dealer you use is a Jackson authorized dealer, and make sure they take responsibility for proper fitting. Also confirm turnaround times for initial order and redo.

* Good Luck!.

True. I know of people who visit skating specialists while visiting countries for other reasons. But at the moment getting visas to the U.S.A. is harder than a few years past, so it's even more unlikely. Which is why I didn't list the podiatrist names in question.

Even if you are very careful to trace your own feet the ways people recommend, bending down to do so changes your weight distribution, and likely changes the angles your feet make with the floor. And if another non-expert does the tracing, there are many mistakes that can be made. E.g., people often want you to make one tracing around the outside of the foot with a vertical pen, and another that is angled inwards as far as you can. But I think the second part would be hard to do, because it isn't clear how much pressure to use. And that doesn't really give you the full shape of the bottom of the foot. The reality is that really good skate fitters get trained by the specific boot manufacturers or their reps, to fit their particular brand of skate. And even so they don't always get a great fit.

Skiing has a lot of similarities to skating, in terms of the importance of snugness of fit. But most skiers don't do figure skating moves. Even the freestyle skiing jumps and spins look different to me - perhaps more like gymnastics? So muscle use in the two sports is significantly different. And AFAIK, most ski boots don't have heels as elevated or feet as bent as most figure skates. This may be out of date but ski fitters often use heat molded cork insoles to fit the space between the feet and downhill ski boots, which sounds very good to me. But molded cork insoles haven't been popular in skates - perhaps there is a good reason? (E.g., the snow cushions jump landings more than the ice, so a hard insole might be better?) Cross country skiers sometimes use liners inside very high end boots that are heat shrinked around the feet, to make a good fit, though that is pretty expensive too. But I think most cross country skiers use looser fits than most figure skaters. And those liners aren't as high as figure skates - though the heat shrinkable inline skate liners used with some high end inline skates are...

I think some ski shops do custom ski boot foot castings. But, ski shop technicians probably haven't been trained by the boot manufacturers to fit the particular brands of figure skate boot you want, which would worry me.

I'm sure the best ski shop technicians are good at their jobs, and some of them work with skates too. (I used to know one who worked on skis, hockey skates, figure skates, and speed skates, as well as inline skates and bicycles. I used him for my skates for a while.) Plus they have boot presses that are more than strong enough to stretch skates. And heat molding ovens, though I don't know if the temperature ranges are the same as for skates. But there is a risk they might not be as good with skates. (BTW, sharpening skis with metal edges is apparently a lot different than sharpening ice skates.) I guess you could talk to those ski shop technicians and ask how much experience they have with skates (especially figure skates), and comfortable they are doing such fittings. You might get lucky and find one who figure skates, and has some idea what they are doing.

Do any of you have more info than that on the differences between fits that ski shops and and skate shops make?

Also, as with skates, some ski fitters are better than others. And fits by the good ones are pretty expensive too.

You maybe don't have a lot of good options...
