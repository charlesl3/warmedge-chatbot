You should at least consider wearing protective gear. I wear a helmet, wristguards, and pads for knees, elbows, hips, and tailbone. There are effective, compact, lightweight options that aren't too hulky and that don't interfere with skating maneuvers. It is important to learn how to fall properly (when possible); but sometimes sudden surprises do happen.

FYI, some wrist guards are dangerous to use on ice. The ones with the metal or plastic bumps over the wrist can slide outward and cause a dislocated shoulder. I used to use them, until a skating coach enlightened me.

As far as fear of falling, ask yourself what specifically frightens you. I have had a true phobia because of a past injury, and it wasn't until the last episode that I realized it isn't the falling that bothers me, nor even the actual possible injury, it is the fear that no one will be there to help me if I get injured. As it turns out, I had a latent fear due to a non-skating situation, which I had never connected to the skating phobia. Now that I know specifically what the fear is, I just make sure there are certain people nearby when I'm skating (I skate sometimes on empty or near empty sessions).

To not fall, you have to learn more advanced elements, such as one-foot gliding, skating backward, forward crossover, backward crossover, three-turns, mohawk turns, and probably jumps. Why? Because these elements put you in "unstable" positions, positions you are not comfortable with. When you learn these elements, you basically learn how to handle those positions. When you feel like you are going to fall, you know you have been in this position before and you now know how to recover from it. Learning to jump helps the most. If you think about it, a jump is basically a fall from the air. It might sound counterintuitive, but learning how to jump made me not afraid of falling anymore.

I used to fall at least once or twice every session when I started skating. But now, if I just skate around, I don't fall anymore, unless the ice is super bad, like there is a big hole in it, and even that I still rarely fall. Also wear protective gear, especially knee pads. I can't count how many times they have saved me.

But the goal shouldn't be "to not fall". I think it should be a given that if you skate, you will fall. After all, the skaters I see at the rink that fall the most are the advanced freestyle skaters practicing doubles and triples. The original posted goal is "to not be afraid of falling". Which is a proper goal, along with "to minimize injury from a fall when a fall inevitably happens".

ETA: The following is obvious, but maybe worth highlighting. You are most likely to fall when you are learning a new maneuver. As you practice and gain competency, you are less likely to fall during that maneuver. The more maneuvers you gain competency in, the less likely you are to fall overall. At some point, if all you do is perform basic maneuvers you are competent in, falls will be rare (except for problems with the ice, other skaters crashing into you, ....). But the great part of skating is that there is a constant stream of new maneuvers to learn.

I completely understand the fear of falling. I had a very, very bad fall on the ice which led to a very serious and gruesome injury that haunts me to this day. It took me 2 years to be able to just stand on the ice again without hyperventilating.

Once I found the courage to start skating again, I had the very same experience. I was sweating just thinking about falling. Personally, what helped me get more comfortable with the notion of falling was having someone help me through some controlled falls (literally, what they teach the little kids learning to skate). I would not have wanted to fall on my own at that point and felt safer knowing someone was there. Even though I had skated previously, I really needed the emotional crutch of going through this with someone. I didn't want to do it at first but when I did, I found out it falling wasn't so bad.

This may just be a trick my mind is playing on me, but when I got better fitting boots (no heel slippage, really felt that they were an extension of me), I felt more secure and less worried that a scary fall was going to happen. I also take precautions (taping and bracing) to make sure my injured knee is as stable as possible (making the possibility of falling less scary).

Also, I finally had a totally unexpected fall (ran over some debris on the ice) and was totally fine. It showed me that falling does not equal injury.

Recognize that the fear does not go away immediately. Don't expect to go to the rink tomorrow and be 100% ok - I'm definitely still scared but am working on it and its no longer a crippling fear. For me, the more practice I have and the more confident I am in my basic skating, the less scared I am of falling because I don't feel like a deer on a frozen pond when I get out there.

I find that a lot of times the anticipation is worse than the actual event. I used to be so scared of bees before I got stung and would literally launch into a panic attack every time I saw one, convinced that if I got stung it would be THE WORST pain in the world. Finally, I did get stung and it really sucked, but it wasn't nearly as bad as what I had made it out to be in my head.

I feel like there are 2 different kinds of falls - unexpected freak falls, and falls that you take when you’re working on something new, and are somewhat prepared for. In the first category, this will at happen to everybody occasionally. A couple weeks ago I saw someone working on gold dances, who was just doing an inside mohawk as part of warmup laps, fall off the back of her blade and slide halfway across the rink. Because of falls like this, everyone who skates must be prepared with falling technique. It doesn’t matter how careful you are or what you’re working on, it could be caused by something external like a rut in the ice or dodging another skater.

For the second category of falling, I feel like it’s a matter of preference or learning style on how much you are averse to this or not. A lot of adults understandably hate falling, and aren’t that aggressive in pushing their boundaries as a result, which is totally fine. This kind of fear isn’t at all uncommon, and for the OP, at this stage in their learning process, I would suggest just getting comfortable on the ice, learning how to fall properly, and not getting too hung up with pushing outside their comfort zone. It will come naturally with time.

Believe it or not, feeling unstable is not necessarily a bad sign when you are learning a new skill---it is a normal part of the learning process and it just means that you have not mastered the mechanics of the skill. Think of it this way: learning a skill means learning how to control that instability. Control is what gives you confidence.

To add on to jf2's Category #2 falls, I personally find it helpful to focus my mind on what's the correct technique I need to execute, which takes my mind off any "what if I fall" thoughts. Doing so will not completely eliminate falls, but it does increase my control and reduce the risk of falling if the element is executed correctly.

When I do fall, 99% of the time in a non-injurious way with only a bruised ego, I focus on identifying what errors I made that caused the fall, and remember to correct the error in future---so that I won't commit the same fall-inducing mistake again.

Disclaimer, there's also a "bad" kind of instability, maybe caused by bad technique and poor control, maybe from attempting something beyond what you're ready for, maybe due to physical problems like fatigue, dizziness, illness, etc. In this case, real injuries could happen, so don't try anything stupid!

Disclaimer: I don't care about being fashionable. For me, it's safety first. Often people are self-conscious or embarrassed about gearing up (or think they need protection for body part A, but can get away with no protection for body part B) ... until they have a bad injury; then they see the light and become a convert.

(a) It's important to go to a shop with a knowledgeable salesperson to guide you. Protective gear comes in a wide variety of forms. It's essential that it fits YOU; else it won't function properly, or it will be so uncomfortable that you will stop wearing it. So you need to try it on. What works for your coach or another skater or other recommender might not work for you.

(b) Number one priority should be head protection. I wear a hockey helmet. Sleeker helmets are the ones designed for snowboarding. DO NOT wear a bicycle helmet (especially the ones with pointy tips fore and aft). Some people refuse to wear helmets, and wear gear that look like hats or headbands. Haven't a clue how effective those are.

(c) For elbow pads and kneepads, I wear those designed for volleyball. I'm a guy. The kneepads are covered by pants, and the elbow pads are covered by a jersey or fleece. I know a few women who skate in pants and a long sleeve top or jacket and wear similar gear. The gear is not visible. One woman I know skates in tights and a skirt. She covers her kneepads with some sort of leggings that extend above the knees.

(d) For wristguards, take a look at these:

https://www.rollerblade.com/usa/products/bladegear-xt-wristguard/?gen=28

They do not have the pronounced V-splint of traditional wristguards (the V-splint can cause you to slide uncontrollably). I wear these over thin black leather dress gloves and they are not blatantly noticeable (the leather also reduces sliding).

(e) For tailbone and hip protection, I've made my own pads. I'll leave it to others to discuss commercial pads. There are some designed to be discreetly worn under tights.

Bicycle helmets are designed for a single-shot, high-speed, high-impact collision. But in general this is not true for hockey helmets. I have an intermediate-grade hockey helmet, and it's designed with three layers of protection: a hard outer shell to reduce the risk of skull fracture in the event of a high-impact collision, an intermediate plastic honeycomb matrix to absorb shock in intermediate-impact collisions, and an inner foam liner to absorb shock in low-impact collisions. The intermediate-grade units are lighter than the low-end and high-end units. They are sculpted to reduce weight and are ventilated. An adjustable ratchet headband and an adjustable chinstrap provide a comfortable fit, while holding the helmet securely in place in the event of a collision. The light weight and symmetric geometry create no negative influence on my spins. I don't jump, but I know a couple of guys who do wear a hockey helmet while doing jumps ... I'm talking about adults who skate for fun, not elite competitors.
