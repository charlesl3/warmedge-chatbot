I've been reading up on the US competitive structure out of curiosity, and I've got several questions:

1. Juvenile/Intermediate have age restrictions for the qualifying competitions (regionals/sectionals/etc), but novice/junior/senior do not. Does that mean adults can and do compete? Obviously, they won't be named to the Olympic team because they're too old, but do some of them manage to rank high against the kids or is it impossible?

2. In a competitive environment, at what age is a child expected to have their double axel? By age 12 so they can compete in Juvenile at Regionals? At what point are they expected to have all of their triples before they're considered "too old" for the competitive track and instead are encouraged to move into the non-qualifying track?

3. Can someone point me to the ISU's age requirements for international competitions? How does that factor in to being "too old"? Like, perhaps you're an amazing teenager or young adult who managed to score really high at US Nationals, but ISU's age limits (too young or too old) stops you?

4. So the kids track has regionals/sectionals, and the adult track only has sectionals? Are they required to have gone thru sectionals before they go to the US Adult Nationals?

5. So adult sectionals is Adult Silver thru Adult Masters Senior level? Is the "Masters" just a word they added? It's confusing for me because it makes me think they are a "Masters" candidate (age 50+) instead of a normal Adult candidate. I know there are four age categories for adults. Do they ever combine these categories if there aren't enough participants at the sectional or national level? Or is that never a problem and they always have plenty of people?

6. I noticed Regionals is labeling things like "juvenile girls", "novice ladies", "juvenile boys", "novice men". Seems like you become a "lady" and a "man" at the Intermediate level? But Juvenile and lower is "girls" and "boys"? Is that because the Intermediate level are usually teenagers?

Arwen17 said:
1. Juvenile/Intermediate have age restrictions for the qualifying competitions (regionals/sectionals/etc), but novice/junior/senior do not. Does that mean adults can and do compete? Obviously, they won't be named to the Olympic team because they're too old, but do some of them manage to rank high against the kids or is it impossible?
Click to expand...

It depends what you mean by adult. There are skaters over 21 competing at novice through senior levels, but in most cases they have just continued training and competing since childhood and adolescence and eventually reached adult age.

If you mean skaters who started as adults or who had skated at middle levels as kids, took off a decade or two, and then returned to competition, I know one of each who passed the intermediate and/or novice tests as adults and competed in club competitions (and maybe regionals in the latter case) as novices. Their skill levels were respectable but on the low end of average compared to the rest of the field. Especially for ladies, the fields are so deep that the likelihood of any given skater of any age getting as far as sectionals let alone nationals and internationals is slim. The best adult starters or returners might fit in with the average skaters at their level but not with the Nationals-bound.

Occasionally skaters who had reach national or higher levels retire from competition in their teens or early 20s, do other things with their lives, and then come back in their late 20s or 30s. They may be able to get back to the level they had been when they left, but the standards of the rest of the field will likely have passed them by.

Some ice dancers, including elite dancers, compete standard track well into their 20s or 30s, possibly with some years off in between.

Occasionally an older male skater, most likely a coach who never left the sport, will team up to compete in pairs with a younger lady who hasn't yet found and may never find a partner closer to her own age.

Because the fields for these disciplines are smaller than singles, it is possible for such a team to get as far as nationals even with "low average" skills for their levels.

2. In a competitive environment, at what age is a child expected to have their double axel? By age 12 so they can compete in Juvenile at Regionals? At what point are they expected to have all of their triples before they're considered "too old" for the competitive track and instead are encouraged to move into the non-qualifying track?
Click to expand...

The majority of juveniles at regionals do not have double axels. For that matter, even the majority of junior and senior ladies at regionals do not have double axels, even though it's a required element in the junior and senior SPs. However, yes, skaters who do go on to Nationals at any level usually have double axels, and usually have triples at higher levels including intermediate lately.

Except for the rare skater who starts at an "older" (i.e., two-digit) age and is making fast progress through lower levels in the first year or two of skating but hasn't yet mastered double axel, yes, if you're in your teens and aren't close to landing that jump yet you're probably never going to be an elite singles skater. A talented teen who is much stronger at basic skating and other non-jump skating skills, performance and musical interpretation, etc., might find success in other disciplines even if they can't master the jumps for elite singles.

3. Can someone point me to the ISU's age requirements for international competitions? How does that factor in to being "too old"? Like, perhaps you're an amazing teenager or young adult who managed to score really high at US Nationals, but ISU's age limits (too young or too old) stops you?
Click to expand...

For junior internationals, the ISU age limits are at least 13 as of the previous July 1 and not yet 19 (for girls in all disciplines and for male singles skaters) or 21 (for male pair and dance partners) as of July 1.

For senior internationals, the ISU limit is at least 15 as the previous July 1, with no upper limit.

People do compete at senior internationals in their 30s. But the examples I know of were people who were already international-level skaters and just kept skating or who took some years off and then came back.

4. So the kids track has regionals/sectionals, and the adult track only has sectionals? Are they required to have gone thru sectionals before they go to the US Adult Nationals?
Click to expand...

US Adult Nationals offers championship events for which skaters need to qualify through Adult Sectionals. They also offer open events at each level at Adult Nationals that skaters can just sign up for as long as they've passed the prerequisite tests -- if there are many entries, these will be divided by age. (The championship qualifiers tend to skew toward the younger adults.)

5. So adult sectionals is Adult Silver thru Adult Masters Senior level? Is the "Masters" just a word they added? It's confusing for me because it makes me think they are a "Masters" candidate (age 50+) instead of a normal Adult candidate.
Click to expand...

Confusingly, "masters" means something different for testing (based on age) and for competition (passed at least intermediate tests, usually though not necessarily before reaching adulthood).

I know there are four age categories for adults. Do they ever combine these categories if there aren't enough participants at the sectional or national level? Or is that never a problem and they always have plenty of people?
Click to expand...

Yes. Ladies' events usually have enough entries to split them by age. Men often do not, and pairs and dance never do, although there has also been a "Centennial Dance" event for older teams.

6. I noticed Regionals is labeling things like "juvenile girls", "novice ladies", "juvenile boys", "novice men". Seems like you become a "lady" and a "man" at the Intermediate level? But Juvenile and lower is "girls" and "boys"? Is that because the Intermediate level are usually teenagers?
Click to expand...

Essentially, yes.

Yes, they do all the time. Most of them are elite skaters that are returning to competition, though. Dennis Phan qualified and skated at nationals for senior men at age 32, after doing very well at junior level when he was younger. Deanna Stellato is 34 years old, and also returned to skating after a long time off. She and Nate Bartholomay represent the US in international competitions. If the US had 2-3 spots they would probably be going to the Olympics.

2. In a competitive environment, at what age is a child expected to have their double axel? By age 12 so they can compete in Juvenile at Regionals? At what point are they expected to have all of their triples before they're considered "too old" for the competitive track and instead are encouraged to move into the non-qualifying track?
Click to expand...

The kids who qualify through to nationals generally will have a double axel at Juv level. Also, many of them are younger than 12. However, at many regionals, there are many skaters there who don't have 2a, even in intermediate. Many skaters who aren't likely to qualify to nationals do keep competing in qualifying events. Nobody will tell them they can't enter. Skaters who like to compete, whether they have competitive level jumps to reach nationals or not, will typically continue to enter both qualifying and non qualifying competitions. It isn't necessarily that much easier to place well at a big club competition than it is at regionals - its the same skaters that show up, just maybe half as many.

3. Can someone point me to the ISU's age requirements for international competitions? How does that factor in to being "too old"? Like, perhaps you're an amazing teenager or young adult who managed to score really high at US Nationals, but ISU's age limits (too young or too old) stops you?
Click to expand...

They usually don't even have international events for skaters at lower level than Junior.

4. So the kids track has regionals/sectionals, and the adult track only has sectionals? Are they required to have gone thru sectionals before they go to the US Adult Nationals?
Click to expand...

Yes, there is only sectionals and nationals for adult qualifying/championship competition. For Championship events (Such as, Adult Gold Ladies Championship), you are required to qualify to nationals via sectionals. However, there are non-qualifying events you can also sign up for at both sectionals and nationals.

5. So adult sectionals is Adult Silver thru Adult Masters Senior level? Is the "Masters" just a word they added? It's confusing for me because it makes me think they are a "Masters" candidate (age 50+) instead of a normal Adult candidate. I know there are four age categories for adults. Do they ever combine these categories if there aren't enough participants at the sectional or national level? Or is that never a problem and they always have plenty of people?
Click to expand...

The age categories are ONLY for nonqualifying events, and they do combine if there aren't enough skaters - but this mostly occurs at the older end of the range, and for men. If you're doing a qualifying, AKA championship event, all ages are always combined, and if you win that, you are the adult champion of every age group. For example, you can compete at sectionals twice, once in Adult Gold Ladies Championship, and also in Adult Gold Ladies age group I, for example. Only the placement in the championship event at sectionals can qualify you to enter the championship event at nationals. However, anyone can sign up and go compete at nationals in Adult Gold Ladies age group I. I know this can be very confusing.

Click to expand...

Well, what it takes to make it to sectionals and nationals depends the strength of the field.

The minimum jump content needed to pass the juvenile freeskating test is single axel. The minimum needed to pass the senior test is double lutz and two different double-double combinations.

Many skaters go to regionals as soon as they can pass the test. Or if they have average jump content, which really for all these levels is about the same as the senior test minimums. Just competing in the qualifying competition is the big accomplishment for most skaters, even if they don't expect to qualify for anything higher. But it's their opportunity to measure themselves against almost all the other competitive skaters at their level in their region.

For girls, especially at lower levels in larger regions, there are qualifying rounds, so just getting to the final round at regionals is a major accomplishment.

Looking at South Atlantics, one of the larger/stronger regions that held its regionals last week:

There were 6 juvenile qualifying rounds with 18-20 skaters each. So well over 100 juvenile girls.

Twenty-eight qualified for the final round. Of those 28, according to the published protocol approximately half attempted at least one double axel. The top 4 skaters (i.e., those who qualified for Easterns) all attempted two, and 3 of those 4 landed both cleanly.
There were surely a few other skaters who didn't make it out of qualifying rounds who did attempt double axels but either didn't succeed and/or were deficient in other areas. I don't have time to go through all the qual round protocols right now.

Looking at the junior ladies' short program, where double axel is required:

All 16 skaters attempted the required element, but only the top 4 completed it successfully. Most of the others had downgrades, and even those who rotated had all or mostly -3s and -2s, so falls or other serious errors.

Double or triple lutz is required as the junior SP solo jump this year. At South Atlantics, only the top 4 junior ladies attempted 3Lz and only top 2 succeeded.

The jump combo can be 2-2, 3-2, or 3-3. Half of them opted for the 2-2, and the majority of the remainder who attempted a triple did not succeed.

Double axel is pretty much the calling card to elite levels. Most skaters will never fully master it if they are even able to attempt it.
That doesn't mean that skaters who are unlikely to get out of regionals this year or ever should not be going to regionals.
Same as with junior or senior skaters from smaller countries who go to Junior Worlds or Europeans with slim hope of making the cut to the free program let alone contending for medals or top 10. There is value and prestige just in participating at that level.

If you can pass the tests, sure.
Usually skaters want to fit in with their peers. An average/high average intermediate is usually capable of passing the senior tests. So often as they get older they will pass more tests and move up in levels even if their actual skill level doesn't increase much. Usually the overall skill (the double jump quality, the basic skating, the presentation) does gets stronger, even if the skater is unable to add more jumps to their repertoire.

So you'll rarely see an average 12-year-old competing junior or senior, because they want to compete with the other average 12-year-olds. If a 12-year-old is far above average and has triple-triple combinations, then she might move up.

And 17-year-old without double axels or triples may move up to junior or senior just to keep up with her agemates and to finish the testing stream before she goes off to college.

At 18, intermediate is no longer an option so she'll have to move up to at least novice if she wants to keep competing.
