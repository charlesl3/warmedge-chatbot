Hello!
I have a question that's been troubling me since I started skating; so I thought I'd ask. Yes; it's the age question.
I skated at age 10, made it up to Basic 5, and I left skating for financial reasons and sorta never came back to it until I was 17, which was about 11 months ago. So, I did have prior knowledge of skating as well as experience, just not incredibly advanced. I have also rollerbladed throughout my teens which also gave me a boost in ice skating, I think. I'm 18 now (just turned in July) and I can do a toe loop, half flip, one-foot spin, forward and backward crossovers, waltz jump, side toe hop, bunny hop, forward three turns, forward spirals on both edges (although my left ones are lower than my right) and back outside three turns. I'm currently working on a Salchow, half Lutz, scratch spin, back inside threes and backward spirals, and a few more elements like that. I'm going to try to swap to a private coach so that they could prep me for the Pre-Preliminary MIF and Freeskate tests. And I really, really want to compete at Regionals some day (and hopefully sectionals.).
Here is my specific question: am I too old to compete in the standard track?
I know that age limits stop at Novice level, within the U.S.
I figure that at my pace, I realistically would not be passing my novice test until I was at least 20 or 21. And of course my ultimate goal is to compete as a Senior, but I would start competing at Novice as soon as I could because I would just want to get as much competing experience as possible. But would it be goofy for a 21 year old to be competing as a Novice? Is that even heard of?
I am aware of the adult track, but it's honestly not my thing. I am an ambitious person and I don't really feel that I have the physical limitations a person, say, in their forties might. I'm only 18. I am short (5'1" ish) and slim (like 97 lbs, and almost no curves!) and I would call myself "in shape". So I feel that I am capable of eventually learning doubles, triples, etc, the same as a young teen would, because I'm basically their size. I want to skate at the same rate of difficulty as they do and proceed as such. I feel kinda young to be among the Adult skaters. Just wanted to clarify that.
Anyways. Could I get some opinions? Thank you so much!!!:thumbsup:

I know a couple of skaters who have competed standard-track novice in their 20s and 30s, one of them at regionals (she had gotten up to juvenile level as a kid) and one of them only at club competitions, then mostly competed in adult events.

It is possible, but very rare.

It takes a lot of time on the ice and hard work to get to novice level or above. The novice moves test has the lowest passing rate, and learning and staying consistent on the harder doubles and double-double combinations is tough with non-skating adult time commitments and even an athletic adult body.

So by all means you can set competing at regionals as a goal, and start working toward it now. As time goes on, and the rest of your life goes on, you'll see whether you're on track to achieve that goal or whether at some point you want to readjust your goals.

Open Juvenile will probably a step along the way.

Best of luck, and enjoy the journey regardless of what the end result turns out to be.

Unless you are skating a couple hours a day and with a coach a couple times a week, moving from no test level to novice freeskate in 4 years is pretty unheard of - it's not just doing the jumps, it's doing them at a passing level for the judges and skating skills. For example, most kids take about 9months to a year to pass intermediate moves. That being said, I think with enough ice time, money, being athletic and fearless and lots of professional coaching, anything is possible. You can do regionals and qualify just by passing the freeskate test. Anyone can sign up if they are at the correct level. It would be a huge dedication. And realize at your age, most skaters are quitting and moving on to college/careers/etc, so you would be competing against 12-18 year olds. But it is your goals that matter - do what makes you happy.

I think you are DRASTICALLY over-estimating your progress and how fast you will make it to the Novice level. If you were already up to your Axel in 11 months of skating, I'd think it was slightly more possible, but the fact that after nearly a full year your highest jump is only a toe loop...

You have to be realistic. It has taken you a year to get to a toe loop - one of the easiest of the single jumps. There are six jumps. To compete at Novice in the US I'm guessing doubles are a minimum, and then you're talking about getting to Sectionals? I would wager that at least one triple would be needed to manage that. You're talking about going from one single jump to all double jumps and potentially a triple in three years. Stop, take a moment, and look at that sentence again. And that's just the jumps, leaving aside the difficult MITF patterns, the spins and general skating skills. I don't know a lot about US tests but even I know that the Novice MITF test is brutal.

Even the top skaters who start as little kids take years to get to Novice - a lot more than four years. You've let your imagination run away with you.

It's true that some skaters can get to a high level very fast. One of the skaters at my rink already had a consistent double axel and was working on triples after about 6 years. But she is young, tiny, muscular, has a strong drive, lots of lessons/practice, and a load of natural jumping talent. I also have heard of skaters passing all their moves tests in only about 4 years. This is not the norm though and it is especially not the norm for people who start at an older age. If you want to be able to do some doubles one day that can be achievable, but I would expect that journey would take you beyond 20 or 21 years old.

It is possible that you could compete at regionals one day, because in order to enter you only have to pass the freeskate test for a level with no age restriction, but you wouldn't necessarily want to skate at regionals though, because it's expensive for the competition fees, coaching fees, travel fees, custom/customized costume, and choreography that you would need to keep up with the other girls competing at regionals. You would also need more technical ability than the novice freeskate test requires, otherwise you are guaranteed to finish at the bottom in the qualifying round. So at that point you have to wonder if its worth the expense, it might just be better to skate at non-qualifying competitions in your state.

I would say that a good goal would be to pass the USFSA tests. You will gain a good proficiency in skating from that while being given goals to help to continue on through the levels. If you want to do this you need a private coach and I would recommend getting an experienced coach, that has taken a lot of students through the levels (competition and tests), even if they are a little more expensive. Having a good private coach can save you a lot of time and agony. One of the reasons you have taken a bit longer to get all your single jumps is probably because you don't have a private coach, in my experience skaters get so much more out of a 30 minute private lesson than a 45 min-1 hour group lesson.

Lastly, I want to encourage you to keep skating and trying to achieve your goals. From my personal experience I think your timeline is a little unrealistic. I'm not saying it's impossible, but when you are older you will face three big problems with skating. Time, money, and physical ability. Can you skate at least 4/5 days a week, for two hours a day, and have about 2-3 hours of private lessons per week? IMO that is a bare minimum for skaters skating at a high moves test/double jump level. Also recognize that at that level you will likely need to have at least 2 private coaches, because at this level coaches tend to specialize. Meaning someone is best to use for jumps/spins and another for moves and possibly someone separate for choreography. It can get even more complicated than that, some coaches are decent for everything up to a higher level but IMO thats harder to find. If you are able to make time for that, there is cost to consider. Initially starting out you can do basic skills and the freeskate levels fairly cost friendly, after that it starts going up a lot and at a certain point you have to decide if you are in or out. Because like I mentioned earlier to get to a novice freeskate level, even just to pass the test you'd need to put in thousands of dollars per year. I'd say at least a few thousand. For a test costume and choreography are less important so you could save money there. The last thing is physical ability, this summer I was interning in a different state and I was working with a wonderful jump coach who is a friend of one of my coaches back home. He cost $120/hour, which was really expensive for me because my coaches at home are $90/hour. But anyways I remember him telling me that he believed anyone could learn double jumps if they want to. That is also a personal belief of mine as well. Triples are different. I think some people just don't have the ability or sometimes the proper coaching for it no matter their age. The other problem is that for those who do have the ability you still need to start within a certain age range. Trying to learn a triple when you are in your 20s would be so difficult as far as time, money, and effort, but also harmful to your body. Triples beat you up, even doubles are harsh on the body, it comes to a point where you have to wonder why you would put yourself through that when there is not really anything you can do with it. If you want to do a nice program and look like a high level skater, doubles will suffice. No one outside of the sport really even knows the difference and you can pass all the USFSA freeskate tests without a triple.

I started skating as an older teen just like you (16 years old). Although I wish I could have started when I was younger and done the whole regionals thing, I wasn't really interested when I was younger and I was pretty afraid. I am 22 now and close to taking novice moves in the field and I hope to take the freeskate before year end. I also am hired by my rink as a junior coach and I get to teach kids in the group lessons, that I once started out in. I am like you where I am slim and look younger than my age. My goal is to pass senior moves and junior/senior freeskate. After that i'll probably have to force myself to severely cut back because its so expensive and I don't even compete anymore. I wouldn't have been able to do this without the reluctant financial help from my parents. If you can afford to do it I completely encourage you to do it because I love skating and I am so glad to be able to do it, I know I will stay involved in the sport in different ways throughout my life. You will get to meet and work with a diverse set of talented people. Also, I recommend looking into Adult Nationals. They have two levels one has the words intermediate/novice ladies and the other has the words junior/senior. These two are qualifying levels you have to qualify at adult sectionals and those who qualify get to go to nationals, I believe there are age limits to these levels and the people in them are more advanced and do double jumps. I've even seen a few do the double axel in the junior/senior level. You need the USFSA tests to be able to compete in these and if you place at sectionals or nationals you technically have a title. So if you really want to compete i'd look into that, you can't do it until you are 21 though, but that gives you time to advance. I totally get how you feel with the two old to be a regionals skater, but to young to be an adult skater. The truth is we are in a grey area, that the sport has not really addressed well in my opinion, where its harder for us because we are older, but we are still fit enough to do the sport at the standard track test level even though they age us out at the more attainable qualifying levels like juvenile and intermediate. It would be nice if they had some regionals-adult nationals hybrid that was for young adults. Because we exist and i've spoken to other skaters in their older teens/young 20s who would like to compete but we don't want to compete with little girls and are not permitted into certain levels we have the skills for because of our age.

Both Juveniles and Intermediates have age cut offs. There is a category called "Open Juvenile" where you are required to do all the same skills as Juvenile but all skaters are over 13 years old. "Intermediate" also has an age cut off of 18 - I have never heard of an Open Intermediate category but maybe one exists or maybe the age issue only comes into play at Regionals.

In terms of "Qualifying" Regionals where an individual skater qualify for Sectionals, it is only for Juvenile and up. "Open Juvenile" and down are part of the "Non-Qualifying" Regionals pyramid.

We had an adult skater (early 30s and when she was on the the ice, she looked in her late teens) at our rink who competed at the Novice level at Regionals for several years in a row. So to answer your original question, it has been done before. But I think it is extremely rare. Who would be your competitors? The really good Novice skaters typically are very young girls - 13 or 14. The girl who won Nationals was "old" at 16. Probably the "average" novice skater closer to 16-18.

It is great that you have such high aspirations for yourself. If you are asking "what are the odds of success at my age?", that is a fair question, and the other posters have rightly explained how and why the odds are stacked against you. I would add two things.

First, beware of optimism bias and the planning fallacy. Unlike at the school going age, once you get out of high school, life gets more complex: college, job, family, friends, marriage, kids, etc., not to mention injury and financial strain. That's what happened to me; since starting skating at 17, every time I almost get my axel, life intervenes, usually in the form of a strategic life decision to devote my energies to non-skating activities. Now, 15 years on, I still haven't gotten my axel, but am still happily skating and channeled my competitive spirit to my career instead.

So, the second thing is that I would urge you to instead consider the question "What role do I want skating to have in my life, now and in the future?" Because your life over the next several decades will be more than just skating. If you fail to achieve your goal, as statistically you most likely will, what next? Would you be glad to have taken the journey regardless of the amount of sacrifice going into it? Would you have a back up plan, after investing all that time and money at the expense of developing other aspects of your life and career?

These are important things to consider. Even more important is to gather information and different opinions in order to make a sound decision for yourself. Good luck!

Firstly, I think it's great you are motivated and really love skating. Having lofty goals is great but I think it's also important to make smaller ones too. The unfortunate reality of skating is that it is expensive and time consuming. As an adult skater I skate 6-8 hours a week and the expense basically eats up my disposable income. Ice time, lessons, and testing fees add up, and I say this as someone who has a good job and a graduate degree. To reach senior level takes a lot of time, money and natural talent. And the senior test is a lot easier than the actual competition standards. I think doubles are definitely a realistic goal with hard work but triples require not only time, money, and effort, but a certain degree of natural talent. I know a lot of great skaters who started young who never mastered triples no matter how hard they worked. The other thing to consider is that your rate of progress is going to slow as skills get harder. When I returned to skating as an adult a year and a half ago I got back the singles I had as a child fairly quickly and learned the lutz but the axel has thus far been a 6 month pain in the you-know-what. I can only imagine doubles are even more challenging (the only double I have even barely started working on is the double sal and lets just say it has been comedy gold).

So that said, I think it's great to dream big, but also good to have more realistic goals too- start working on the tests and see where it goes. Even if you test standard you can still compete in the adult track when you age into it. Adult has masters levels when you get past gold which encompass intermediate/novice and junior/senior. The important thing is loving skating, if you have that, everything else is extra.
