Hi all,

I'm left wondering about how the judges arrive at GOE numbers, after having watched Europeans with the more detailed scoring box on the screen. I do understand that an element can get up to 3 extra points from the Base Value if it is done particularly well, or have up to 3 points taken away for less than expected quality. What I don't get is how they come up with the exact numbers. I mean, I could see if they, for example, added 1 point for a very nicely done jump. Or, I even could see how they might come up with .50 either way. But I kept seeing things like . 34, or .56----seemingly odd numbers. Also, it all seems kind of arbitrary to me anyway, even if the numbers themselves were less odd. Now I'm certainly no expert, but if I see a jump let's say, that is entered into with some speed and not with a long set-up, then the rotations are tight and fast, and the jump is reasonably high and covers some distance, then is landed smoothly with the free leg nicely extended, then I think, Wow, that's a great jump! I would then expect to see something approaching a + 3.00 GOE, but from what I see, that rarely happens. (Now, I am definitely willing to admit that I might easily miss some flaw, but frequently I seem to be in agreement with the announcer's assessment. They say things like "beautiful", "text-book", "easy", "look at that glide out", etc. but then a GOE of maybe +.50 goes up!) So what has to happen to score closer to +3??
Anyway, thank you in advance for any enlightenment:luv17:

Hi YagsFan,
First, for the theory, here's the GOE Guidelines
There's a list of bullet points which the judges "should"(!) use to determine the GOE of an element...

Although I think it's a great idea to add that tech box on the TV screen with all the information, the "GOE" part may seem confusing to some: It shows you the (provisional, the judges can still go back and change their GOE marks) points won or lost by the GOE mark. It's not the same as the GOE mark!
For example, if you fall on a 3A and all the judges give you -3, then the GOE box will show "-3". Because the GOE mark is taken over as it is.
For all other triples, there's a factor of 0.7: if you fall on a 3T and all the judges give you a -3, then you'll get 70% of the GOEs as points; that's -2.10., and that's what the tech score box will show on your screen.
For all quads, the factor is 1.33: if you fall on a 4T and all the judges give you a -3, the GOE box will show "-4".
For the 2A, it's just 0.5. -3GOEs across the board mean you lose 1.5 points on GOEs for that element.

I think the commentators are aware of the risk of overwhelming the public with too much information and too many rules, so they just don't address that... I don't know how this could be explained more clearly in the future... except if they suggest to their viewers to go and visit GoldenSkate for answers!!

About the odd numbers you saw: there are 9 judges, the lowest and the highest mark gets taken out, and the final mark is the average of the remaining 7 marks, for each element.
There's also a bonus of 10% for jump elements performed after the halfway point. So a 3F has its base value increased from 5.3 to 5.83. More odd numbers!

You can have a look at the pdf of the judges scores for each event, and see how it all panned out: Click on "judges scores (pdf)" in the category you're interested in. Here's the Euros overview.

I'm a sucker for going through the judges scores pdf after an event, and I usually find some things that annoy or even infuriate me.
At the end of the day, whatever system you give them, they'll find ways to give who they want an advantage, and who they want a disadvantage. Not only through the Program Component Score, but also through the GOEs.
A beautiful 3Lz with arms aloft will give a no-name skater skating in the 1st warm-up group a GOE of 0, when the same jump with the same quality performed by a world champion skating last will get a +2 for it.
Even on things that have a strict rule, the judges often disregard them. Like in the pairs event, when Stolbova did a 3T3T and Klimov a 3T2T, there's a mandatory -2GOE for "different number of rotations". Still, 3 judges gave them a +2GOE for that element! Mathematically, (3-2=) +1 should have been the maximum any judge could give them for that...

The purpose of the GOEs is to reward quality wherever judges see it. The purpose is not to hold up skaters with reputations or skaters without reputations, but just to mark each element as it happens.

What usually happens is that skaters who can do a lot of things well earn high GOEs, score high and place well, and develop reputations based on their skating. And then they usually continue to do a lot of the same skills with similar quality after they're known quantities than before.

Look at Loena Hendrickx's scores in the Euros FS. Her jump scores were largely not in the same league as the medalists, but she scored quite well on spins and step/choreo sequences. Because she executed well.

Let's just say that what's rewarding for me when watching skating is to try to analyze the GOEs and PCS according to my understanding of the rules and standards and figure out what scores I would give in real time. I also like to analyze protocols afterward to see how my assessments compared with the experts. I learn a lot that way, and even more when I was first starting out as a diehard skating fan (back in 6.0 days, when there was even more speculation/mindreading necessary to try to figure out why judges might have scored as they did).

My assumption is that most judges know the rules and try to apply them to the best of their ability, and that there is often room for honest disagreement (that's why a whole panel of judges is needed). If my scores are near the majority, I figure we're all correct. If I'm way off base, then I was probably missing something, and I can learn by trying to figure out what.

If most judges more or less agree with each other and with me and one or two are out of line, I figure those judges either made a mistake in terms of what they saw or what rules they did/didn't apply (they are human after all), or had an unconventional way of interpreting the rules. Or saw what they expected to see, as we all do to some degree.

If none of those explanations make sense to me, and if there seems to be a suspicious pattern in how any given judge is marking as opposed to isolated instances, then I might conclude it was likely they were cheating. But I don't go looking for examples because that's not what's interesting to me -- I'm most interested in analyzing the skating and in how the rules are really supposed to be applied.

And you can't tell when judges are breaking the rules otherwise doing a bad job of judging without a thorough understanding of what a good job looks like. Which is why that's the last place my thoughts go, not the first.

Bad GOEs -- judges who are out of line with the majority for no apparent reason (such as the single -3 for Grm's flip) or clearly not within the rules (such as +2 for an element with an error requiring -2 reduction) -- do happen. They are exceptions and can be noted as such, but spending more time focusing on the errors than on the vast majority of scores that do make sense gives a twisted perspective on the actual quality of the judging. Not to mention automatically jumping to the least charitable conclusions about reasons for those bad scores.
