I can't really answer you. I'm not an expert. But:

BTW I think Jackson Mark V and Aspire XP blades are a fair bit different, in terms of the front of the blade - both the toepick position, and the curvature of the "spin rocker" section between the toepick and the main portion of the blade. From your picture, the main purpose of the Mark V is to make sure you don't accidentally touch your toepick. Which might be fine for a beginner.

Some people claim that the point at which the curvature of the blade changes, and the curvature of the spin rocker, are more important than the main curvature (7' vs 8' rocker radius) itself. In particular, AFAICT, both the Mark V and the Coronation Ace have a point of change fairly far back - maybe almost as far back as the ball of your foot. Whereas the MK Pro has it fairly far forwards. Some people would therefore say that the Coronation Ace blade would be easier to control for a low level skater, whereas the MK Pro requires more finesse. But we haven't seen you skate. That said, a fair number of advanced skaters do like the Coronation Ace. (Some companies claim it is the world's most popular figure skating blade.) But a fair number like MK Pro too.

My impression, from your pictures, is that the Mark V was designed as a beginner blade. You had to rock quite far forwards before the toepick would touch. That made it easy to avoid the toepick. But many beyond the "March & Glide" phase (and since you say you are working on 3-turns, you are beyond March & Glide phase) of figure skating work best if you can use the toepick without trying too hard.

I suspect Diana Delafield is a very experienced skater, who has the subtle control the blades she likes require. So for her, having to rock through that extra distance to the toepick (on the Coronation Ace, and probably even more if she used the Mark V) seems clumsy. Maybe that is what she was talking about when she said it was clumsy. Really good skaters often love blades best that let them do things with very subtle movements.

Have you spoken to your coach about blade choice? She/He/etc. might have opinions based on your skating level and how much control you have transitioning between different parts of your blade. Not all coaches know much about blades, but a lot of them do. If your coach isn't comfortable making a recommendation, could you ask your figure skating director?

Some good skate techs can do this too. If your skate tech hasn't seen you skate, and try to do the things you are trying to do, he might have trouble figuring out what to tell you to get. Could you present him with a video of you trying to do those things?

BTW, I'm not at all sure about the Aspire XP. I think it is meant to be a sort-of beginner blade, but not so much as the Mark V. But Jackson does put it on some boots that were designed for your mentioned skating level, but they are pretty low level. Also, they rust quite easily - I've been running into that problem myself on a pair I tried, even though I wiped them dry after use. Even if I remove the rust, sometimes it comes back during a single skating session. Also, looking at Aspire XP blades of the same size, they had very different shapes straight from the factory, sometimes even between the left and right blade of the same pair. I don't think Jackson/Ultima puts a lot of effort into making them consistent. However, they may be a fair bit cheaper than the other blades you mentioned. (My impressions of cost are based on living in the U.S. - maybe tariffs changes that?) Other people with more experience might disagree, but if you are paying for lessons, especially private lessons, the cost of lessons + ice time might more than justify getting better blades than Aspire XP in the long run. The way I see it, why pay for extra lesson time to compensate for dubious quality blades?

But maybe there are people here who like Aspire XP?

BTW, there are short tailed 7' and 8' rocker blades. There are long tailed 7' and 8' rocker blades.

The Coronation Ace blades you tried - were they new? New or not, most skate techs re-sharpen MK and JW blades, creating a significantly different shape. It is possible that badly sharpened blades (by a poor skate tech) could have a shape that caused you to rock to places you didn't want to be. For example, suppose that the center had a fairly abrupt change of shape. That could cause you to rock from there to either the back or the front of the skate. If you had blades that were badly sharpened, you might not have given Coronation Ace blades a fair trial.

I mention this because in the picture you show, the position of the tail of the Mark V and the Coronation Ace are not all that different relative to the stanchion positions. At least not different enough for me personally to have such strong feelings.

Some opinions about blade shapes, including Coronation Ace, are at

Choosing Figure Skating Blades

scarletskater.wordpress.com

Another issue is that the two boots in your picture look to be rather different lengths, though I'm not sure of that because of the different heel heights. And the boot shapes, in terms of the part your foot rests on, look a lot different. It may be hard to figure out what is causing all the unhappiness generation if you change too many variables at once. Hopefully the expert fitter will put you in boots that work very well for you. Do you still have the Coronation Ace blades? If you could show them to the expert fitter, and he thinks that they were sharpened well, or are correctable, he might be able to have you try them on the new boots too. But you may need some experience with the new boots before being able to make good judgements about blades with them.

Since your skate tech did not tell you to change blades at the same time, he doesn't always tailor his advise for the purpose of maximizing profit, as some techs do. Perhaps that's a good sign?

Aside to Diana: I assume you chose your current coach because you like the way she coaches. Maybe you wouldn't want a coach who only said nice things about your skating, and didn't give you things to improve? I'm sure you know that sharper blades might create fewer shavings and cleaner lines, but perhaps you don't like ultra-sharp blades. I've noticed many skaters don't. They either want the skate tech to slightly dull the blades after sharpening (by running a flat abrasive stone against the edge, sometimes called deburring, though unless the tech uses a fine grain wheel, there may not actually be a sideways facing burr) or they do it themselves, one way or another. But, have you tried sharper blades? (I'm not just talking about radius of hollow - I'm mostly talking about that so-called deburring operation, which also rounds off the edge a bit - sometimes more important than ROH.)

I sometimes like very sharp edges, though it means I can't take even a single step off-ice onto a rubber mat without blade guards. So I've varied a little about this. Ultra sharp edges are great on very cold ice, or not-so-smooth ice where you need to cut through the bad stuff on top, or ice so warm you need to cut through the slush. But maybe proper weight/blade alignment is more important? I think one probably won't skid if the vector sum of your weight under gravity and centrifugal force aligns with the blade direction. But your coach can probably explain that better. And you probably already know it.

This is off topic. But I want to point out that dulling (rounding off) edges and deburring edges are not the same operation, they are separate and distinct operations. That is, if an edge has a burr to begin with (created, e.g., from sharpening or skating), and if you wish to remove the burr, the final edge can be duller or sharper than the initial edge, depending on the tool and technique used to remove the burr. Similarly, if an edge does not have a burr to begin with, you can manually touch up the edge, and the final edge can be duller or sharper than the initial edge, depending on the tool and technique used for touch up.
