First of all is it an "air brace"? Because that's what it sounds like from your description because I have worn plenty of those from ankle injuries while training and competing. The air brace was not for me to wear while in skates, only off ice to support my ankle because my Doctor of Sports Medicine/Orthopedic Surgeon said the skate boot was enough to support it along with a soft sleeve-like brace while on ice. If it is indeed an air brace you are speaking of, you will not be able to wear that in your boot, it won't safely fit.

As far as if this is a good idea...it depends on what you meant above when you said "blew your ankle out" and what the injury exactly was. Depending on what it was, I will let you know that even basic stroking and edge work will put stress on your ankles and really make the tendons and ligaments work hard.

I wish you well in wanting to learn just basic skating. If you and your doctor feel that it would be okay for you to do so, then I don't see why not. Good luck!

If the brace is so big that you'd need to get a different size boot, I would NOT recommend skating in rentals or rec skates and risk hurting yourself. If you need a boot that's bigger just to fit your brace, it will be snug against the brace but won't be snug against the rest of your foot, so it won't adequately support you at all.

If you're very serious about skating, custom boots are a possibility, where the bootmaker can see if they can create a well fitting boot around your foot and ankle and duplicate the support of your brace in the shaft and upper of the boot. You would be surprised what they can do - Harlick made custom boots for Elaine Zayak who was missing 3 toes on her left foot. This would be expensive though - ballpark $750 for just the boots.

Falling is a fact of life in skating - but if you take some learn to skate lessons, you'll learn how to do it safely, and get back up safely. A lot of people wear padding so they don't get hurt. It doesn't really impact the ankles typically if you are wearing well fitted boots. Figure skates are supposed to allow a forward and back bend, but not very much lateral movement. Maybe that does the same thing as your brace - in any case, good boots should do a lot to protect ankles.

Actually I understand that it's easier for beginners to start in figure skates or general recreational skates because hockey blades are more curved, i.e., have less of the blade on the ice at a time, and are therefore harder to learn to balance in.

I've never tried hockey skates myself, so I can't speak from personal experience.

Beginners who are planning to play hockey might start in hockey skates regardless of the difficulty, or else switch over as soon as they get comfortable on the ice.

Of course, someone who has spent years skating in one type of skate would face some challenges adjusting to the other, as in tennisguy's clip.


As Ic3Rabbit says, a good pair of figure skating boots (adequately broken in but not broken down) can provide ankle support on their own or perhaps in combination with a light stretch brace that shoes do not provide, so that may be an option depending on the condition of your ankle.

Is it likely to improve in the future, or is it now as good as it's going to get?

Freestyle blades typically have a rocker radius of 7 to 8 ft, whereas hockey blades typically have a rocker radius of 9 to 13 ft; therefore, hockey blades are typically "flatter" than freestyle blades, not the other way around. On the other hand, hockey blades are typically thinner (measured across the blade from inside edge to outside edge) than freestyle blades (dance blades are typically thinner than freestyle blades; some dance blades may be as thin as some hockey blades).

As a kid, I took 2 8-week sessions and then probably skated about 1-2 a year until I was ~20. I resumed "skating" when my daughter was first learning - I was about 40 at the time.

When I was younger, I only tried figure skates. Years later, I was told that hockey skates were easier so I tried them once.

The shape of the blade behind the rear mount was the problem. Figure skate blades extend a couple of inches behing the back mount but hockey skates curve up directly behind the back mount. So with figure skates I could lean back a bit and still stay upright but when I tried the same thing with hockey skates, I immediately feel back and landed on my behind.

My experience is hockey boots are more comfortable but ice skating blades are more forgiving to the beginner. Hope that makes sense.

Since you had such a serious injury, i would suggest you ask your doctor specifically if it's okay for you to ice skate. It's easy to fall when you're learning and you want to make sure he's okay with it. If he says yes, then ask him whether you need to wear that specific brace while skating or if there's an alternative.

Once you have that information I would suggest you find a skate shop with a really good, experienced fitter (not a teenager who works in the pro shop at your local rink). The last thing you want is skates that are too big: it's much easier to trip and fall.

The fitter should be able to figure out whether it's possible to accommodate the brace, and whether you would need a custom skate. He or she might need to talk to your doctor and the skate manufacturer to figure it out. Then he can tell you your options and the cost, and you can decide if it's worth it. If you don't know a good fitter, someone here might be able to recommend one.
