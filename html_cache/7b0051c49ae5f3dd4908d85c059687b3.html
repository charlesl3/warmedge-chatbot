FWIW, I did both gymnastics and figure skating as a kid. I was not at a high level in either. And my parents somewhat discouraged us from getting too involved in any one sport. At a certain point, I chose to give up skating for gymnastics. I don't really remember why I did it, and I wish I hadn't, for the reason that ifshehadwings said. Skating is something you can do your whole life, and I picked it back up as an adult, whereas gymnastics is probably much harder as an adult. My adult skating has been great. But I do sort of wish I hadn't lost those years from 10-18, when I could have been learning more skills.

I wish there was a more obvious track in skating for kids who aren't going to compete seriously, but want to continue to learn and pursue the sport at a more relaxed pace. I guess maybe testing (as opposed to competing?) is that track. But it seems like coaches push competing a lot.

clairecloutier said:
I wish there was a more obvious track in skating for kids who aren't going to compete seriously, but want to continue to learn and pursue the sport at a more relaxed pace. I guess maybe testing (as opposed to competing?) is that track. But it seems like coaches push competing a lot.
Click to expand...

In the US, currently, skaters can pass tests only and never compete. It's pretty common for skaters to reach around intermediate level and then "test out" to finish their tests before going to college or quitting for other (e.g., financial) reasons. Some will just aim to finish the Moves in the Field tests but not the freestyle tests.

Coaches may push competing because it's what most of their students do so they're used to it, or because they earn more money if skaters take more lessons, or because they think that competing in appropriate events is a good way for skaters to set goals for themselves and test their skills by combining them into programs.

The latter would be a good reason IMO, unless the skater really hates performing (but learning how to perform is something that will serve them well in their post-skating years, even for something like giving work presentations), or is really aiming to quit as soon as possible as soon as they pass certain tests.

But it would be important to have a coach who understands what the skater's goals are and to work with them to navigate the options within the local figure skating scene to achieve it.

ISI competitions have a lot of more recreationally focused events, but many coaches at USFS clubs and rinks have no connection with ISI, there aren't any competitions nearby, and no one knows what's available there.

Within USFS, there is now a "competitive test track" in which skaters are allowed to include only skills that are required on the test for that level, or sometimes one level higher (e.g., Preliminary test track skaters are allowed to include lutzes and camel spins).
That option may be more popular at some rinks, in some geographical areas, than others. It also seems to be more common at lower levels -- I've only rarely seen intermediate-senior test track events at a club competition. Most club competitions do offer all levels of test track, though.

Open Juvenile is a good option for skaters who start late and can pass lower level freestyle tests and at least juvenile Moves, but either don't have any consistent doubles (at least one is needed for the intermediate test) at all or prefer to compete in a less pressured environment without short programs and without having to compete against kids attempting triples.

Then there are also often spin events, jump events, compulsory moves events (for juvenile level and below in place of short programs), which usually take place on half ice without music. And showcase events (dramatic and light entertainment), which focus more on the performance qualities than on technical skills, although good skating quality is always welcome.

None of those types of competitions exist when I was a kid -- and also all the lower-level tests were figures only back then -- so I had no reason to even put together a program of any kind. Which is why I got bored and quit. If there had been some option to compete as a preliminary-level teenager, I probably would have kept skating long enough to get to Open Juvenile level, had it existed back then.

Synchronized skating and Solo dance competition are also popular now. But they involve different skills usually used from different coaches. There would need to be a synchro team or a dance coach nearby.

SmallAminal said:
I think it is good to continue to have conversations with your daughter about how she wants to allocate her (limited) time between her sports and other activities.

I am in the midst of trying to have similar discussions with my son, who just turned 7. In his own 7-year-old way, he wants to do EVERYTHING and he doesn't want to choose. So we actually starting breaking the conversation down into more manageable pieces for him since we says he loves skating as much as he loves tennis and he competes in both (although he says he would rather play golf all time time, but that's not possible in our climate). We talk about what he likes about each one, whether he would be happy just to do one or both "recreationally" or if it is important for him to compete. We also talk about other things he likes to do (like after school science or pottery) and finding out if he would be upset knowing that he can't do some of that because tennis and/or skating gets in the way. I found that giving a concrete example of what his week would look like and what the outcomes could be were helpful. For example, "you can skate every day after school, but that means you won't be able to do pottery or science at school and there is no other way to do those on the weekend. Are you ok with that or do you want to make time for them?"

Also, I don't know if this applies to your situation, but as my son moves up to a new team in competitive tennis, he is *required* to commit to the mandatory practice times and it means 10+ hours a week. Skating, on the other hand, does not require him for a fixed amount of time, so it is possible for him to progress (albeit slowly) in skating by participating at a lower intensity. We talk about how the new tennis level would mean he couldn't skate even as much as he is now, and he wouldn't have time for other activities. We leave open the option to still play a lot of tennis (e.g. 5 or 6 hours a week) but not as part of the team. We also talk about what that means for his opportunities in each sport, etc.

The other thing is that we have these conversations a few times a week and we are having them over a very long period of time. He needs to make some decisions about tennis by August and I find that his feelings on the subject change from moment to moment, so you can't just sit him down and say "decide now!".

I remember when I was a little bit younger than your daughter, I told my mom that I wanted to focus on competitive swimming and that I would give up dance in order to swim more. My mom made me think about it for a long time and ran through all of the repercussions and scenarios with me so that I was sure of what I was doing. It wasn't something she let me pull the trigger on immediately. I guess my point is that you should keep the conversation open and flowing with your daughter and be there to provide some real insights to her without trying to sway her decision. And if she decides she really wants to do both, make sure that she understands that she is necessarily limiting how far she can go in either of them, but there is NOTHING wrong with wanting to be a well-rounded person with varied interests.
