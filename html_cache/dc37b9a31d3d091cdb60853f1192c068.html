Who recommended the current boots for her? My guess is was not the coach.

The Jackson Elite 4200 is ALOT of boot. The "more" boot you have, the more stiffness it provides which in turn, would make the boot heavier. (Hmmm ... weren't stiffness and weight your daughter's complaints). To put the type of skater that benefits from wearing the Jackson Elite 4200 into perspective, I read over the summer that Elizabet Tursynbayeva (Kazakhstan) is wearing them.

Why was I researching this particular boot? My daughter's coach recommended that we get that model for my 11-year old daughter (4 ft 9 and 72 lbs). At the time, my daughter had all of her non-axel doubles for 2+ years and was working on her double axel. Even the technician that fitter my daughter questioned whether it was too much boot for her. We ended up going with this model but opted for the lighter weight version of the 4200 (just introduced May 2016). The 4200s are a great boot if that is where your child is.

Prior to that time, my daughter had the Premiers. My guess is Premiers (or maybe even Competitors) were probably what the coach recommended.

I am also questioning why you are worried about how long the boot will last. Most parents with young skaters agree that we are lucky to get year out of a pair of skater. The issue is not the boot breaking down but the skater's foot growing.

My daughter has never worn EDEAs so I cannot offer any advice on those.

My point to all this - go with what the coach recommends instead of second guessing him/her. My guess is you did not follow the coaches advice with the 4200s and now your daughter is paying the price. Also remember that little kids need less of a boot than larger people - from what I have seen, most boot ratings are for bigger people, not little kids.

There is nothing wrong in buying a second hand pair of boots provided they are at the right level for your child. For about 3 years, I got my daughter's Freestyles off the ebay. I would recommend doing this for little kids because they don't have the weight to break in a new pair of leather boots which can lead to back problems.

The 4200s are a rock hard solid boot. My guess is even after a couple of years, they still have a lot of stiffness (too much for your daughter since she is complaining about them) and they still weight a lot. From my own experience with my daughter and from what you are saying, I think purchasing another pair of used 4200 is a very bad idea.

My impression is you want to go with EDEAs since 1) you have had a bad experience with the used 4200s and 2) you want to re-use the blade. I really don't think #1 is a valid reason to since your daughter is was way overbooted. #2 maybe. Have you asked your coach what model of Jacksons and blade she/he would recommend? Depending on what the coach says, it may be the Jackson boots + blades may be the same price point as a pair of EDEA boots only.

Competitive ice skating is a horribly expensive sport. I believe the most important part of the sport is getting the skater the right pair of boots + blades that match their current skill set. Even if the person has a huge amount of talent and a great coach, the wrong pair of skaters will hold back the skater. Save money by getting your daughter cheaper practice clothes, not on her skates.

My guess is once your daughter get a set of skates that match her current skill set, she will make a lot of progress.

My daughter's coach told us, she needed boot model x with blade y. We were even told that we needed to go to a certain pro shop and speak with technician z. The only deviation we made from what we were told was to get the super light version of the boot model. It turned out the new version was introduced in May 2016 and we happened to go boot shopping the first weekend of May.

The elite 4200 are too much boot for your daughter. If you decide to stay with a Jackson, you should only be considering the premier 2800 which is 1) what your daughter's coach recommends and 2) it is a lighter boot.

I have no insight on special insoles. My daughter has always used the "stock" ones. It may be your daughter needs the insoles extra shock absorbancy due to her current boots. Why? Too stiff of a boot does not allow proper knee bend which cause can undue strain on the lower back - so she could be using the insoles to add "padding" which compenates for too stiff of a boot. Or the insoles may be related more to flat feet.

Given what you said about $$ constraints, used Chorus skates being available, and your coach's boot recommendations, I would check to see if you can get a second hand pair of the Chorus boots and then use the existing gold seals on them.

Your daughter's feet are still going to grow so expect only to get about a year out of this set. This combination should work well for that period of time. Once she outgrows those, then reevaluate her needs.

Lots of people view ice skating parents as "rich" so I have noticed a tendancy of some vendors to upsell the parents of skaters. If you don't watch out, those vendors will bleed you dry with the intent of padding their pockets. That was Karne's earlier point.

Good luck in what you decide. Remember getting the right boots + blades for your daughter's current skill set lays the foundation for the skating.

Insoles or orthotics when appropriate are really important. Moulding doesn't take care of the bottom of the foot. And it seems that many people don't seem to realize this. A good fitter should be able to help with this. So that is another reason not use shop online in your case.

Here's what we've learned with two kids with special fitting needs.

If there is a high arch, there is a risk of overbooting in order to stop pronation. The big empty gap in the instep needs appropriate support that is built for skate boots, but folks often try to compensate with a more rigid boot instead. Standard insoles and orthotics cannot be used as they are intended to move while walking. Supports in skates need to be firmly in place. Some skate brands have orthotic insoles that can be ordered. As Riedell's can't be fitted locally, they are unfortunately out for you, but other brands may offer them too.

Skate orthotics that are individually made are very expensive, likely more than the skates and blades themselves, although they can be used for a size up or two. Unfortunately, specialists who can make orthotics for skates well are rare, and even in elite centres there are usually only one or two who get it right. So, doing the best you can with the manufacturers options is a reasonable choice.

Lastly, if there is a high arch, the blade may need to be offset towards the inside of the boot. Still at right angles to the sole, but with the entire blade shifted in. This is because the midline of balance of weight is different when there is a high arch as I understand.

I am aware of a number of skaters at our club who reached a limit in progress in their jumps that was almost miraculously resolved by remounting the blades and adding a standard skate insole with arch support. The difference was noticeable from the first session after the adjustment. But the fitter who knows how to do work out the correction is a couple of hours away in another city. The coaches just advise families to make the trip when they see this issue developing. It's not an unreasonable expense as compared to time wasted in unsuccessful training or injuries.

As usual for skater's parents, it's important to work out your child's needs to avoid unnecessary expenses, but also to avoid the mistake of being "penny wise, pound foolish".

There seem to be different coaching philosophies around whether jumps should be taught sequentially or in bundles.

And good coaches seem to determine what next based on not only abilities, but also temperament and learning style of a student.

At our club, the axel and easier doubles S and T, often seem to be trained in the harness at once, but in some cases the axel and all doubles are learned at once. The coaches say that when they start consistently landing one out of the harness, the rest come quickly in a domino effect. If your daughter already has a consistent axel, starting the double axel with the rest of the doubles would be consistent with this approach.

So, if something seems to be getting in the way of certain jump, but not others then the coach will be working to figure it out.

If the coach feels there are possible issues with her boots, I would take it seriously. Coaches recognize patterns of problems and, at the level of triples, injuries that come from boots that don't fit well.

BTW While it sounds as though you are in the US, 9 years old and 3 years of skating for a girl seems to me to be in line with coming towards the end of the "learn to train" and heading into the "learn to compete" phase of Skate Canada's long term athlete development model.

Definitely see our kids working through all their jumps in succession in a freeskate session once they're landing them, but with more focus on one or two jumps on a given session. This is especially the case when coaches are trying to bring elements up to the level to be introduced for competition.

While I don't see kids starting to learn all those jumps on the same day, over a phase of harness training, a group of jumps will be added in. Starting additional jumps does not necessarily wait on obtaining one or even a few. Off-ice training will be focusing on adding in the additional rotation.

But coaching techniques definitely vary, and some push the introduction of higher level elements early, several levels above where the skater is competing...

Agreed that triples are not allowed in recreational events until the in the Gold level of the new STARSkate, but with the options for triples and bonuses for combinations being introduced at the Novice and Pre-Novice level on the competitive side, coaches seem to be accelerating the acquisition of jumps when the skater has the potential and learning style to make it work.

The current boot she has is so incredibly stiff that there is no way she can bend her knees far enough to do anything. If you are limited to Edea and Jackson only I would suggest the chorus. It is leather and soft enough for a little girl like your daughter to use. Something as stiff as the Icefly or 4200 may cause shin splints especially in such a petite skater. Also, stiffness ratings are unrelated between brands. I as a rule ignore them. The newest model 4200 is made of a horrible synthetic material that is so stiff that I, a 5' 10'' 160 lb male skater could not use it.

The piano has been suggested by some people. When I tested it I could not feel the ice and my jumps were much smaller. I suspect it is exactly because of the "anti-shock" tech in the skate. My Risport RF-1 is my favorite boot I've ever had. However the Chorus is a good little boot for a small skater and I would get that one if I was in your position.
