Just here to say that buying "online" isn't always the $ saving move that you think it will be.
You still need to have your blades mounted (they may/may not charge you extra for this online) and there are often adjustments that need to be made to the blade mounting after the initial temp mount. Also, if you are looking at a heat moldable boot, you will need to see a skate tech locally who can do that for you and so you'll have to pay again for that (whereas its included when you buy the skates). Also, if you need adjustments OR something goes wrong, a local retailer may be better positioned to help you than an online retailer. There is quite a lot of value in having a bricks and mortar store that will support you as boots often need multiple adjustments or troubleshooting. Just something to keep in mind before you assume that you are saving $200 by buying online.

* That pricing differential is a bit bizarre. I'm in the US, not Canada, so pricing may work differently there. Perhaps other Canadian members in this forum will pitch in with their experiences of purchasing skating equipment in Canada.

* I checked the Canadian website of Jackson Ultima. The list price for the women's Freestyle FS2190, complete with Aspire XP blades, is Can$439.95. I checked 6 Canadian online sites (some charge shipping, some don't; I've not adjusted for shipping). Four are selling them at or close to list price. One has a holiday sale for Can$351.86. Amazon CA does not sell them directly, but various marketplace sellers on that site sell them for rip-off prices. The sellers and prices vary with size. For size 7R, the asking price is Can$1,180.51; for size 7W, the asking price is Can$935.15.

* So what price is the store that you just went to asking? And what price did you find online?

* In the US, for higher-end figure skating equipment (not your department, sporting goods, or discount store variety), you typically do not save money by buying online. Online retailers typically all charge list prices. If there is a significantly lower price on a particular item, then it's typically (1) a discontinued item that the retailer wants to clear out (either the manufacturer has discontinued the item, or the retailer no longer wants to carry the item), (2) an overstocked item that the retailer wants to reduce inventory on, or (3) a limited-time special sale (e.g., holiday, anniversary, or grand opening). There are always odd exceptions, of course: e.g., there once was a large US online seller called Kinzie's (now out of business); it charged list for most boots and blades, but had on-going discounted prices for Paramount blades.

* In the US, most full-time shops (see further discussion below) purchase from wholesalers at discounted prices and sell to end customers at list prices; that's how they can make a profit. But the total price you pay will typically be much less if you buy from a shop than if you buy the equipment online. If you buy the equipment from a shop, the following services are typically included: initial consultation and fitting, heat molding of the boots, additional adjustments of the boots (e.g., stretching and punching out), sharpening of the blades, mounting of the blades, and re-positioning and re-alignment of the blades. If you buy the boots and blades online (or from a distant shop), and have these services done by a local tech, you will have to pay. And the costs can add up rapidly. E.g., the shop that you visited did not charge you for initial consultation and fitting even though you did not buy from her (but maybe this is a prime example of you get what you pay for). The guy I go to charges US$85 for a 90 min appointment. This fee is waived if you buy from him. As another example, first-time sharpening on a new pair of blades you buy from him is free; US$29 otherwise. So before you decide to buy online, check what local service charges are.

* In the US, there are some shops that will charge service fees in addition to list price. But the service fees for equipment you buy from them are typically discounted from regular à la carte service fees. I also know some coaches who sell equipment part time. Often they don't do enough business to get a wholesale discount, so they buy retail and mark it up to make a profit.

* Time to hit pause. Buying the right figure skating equipment is unfortunately complicated and expensive. That's why there are so many posts here concerning equipment.

* Again, compare the total price (boots plus blades plus services) between different sellers (whether in a shop or online). Even at your local shop, ask the lady what she charges for services if you bought the boots and blades elsewhere; and ask her what she charges for the boots and blades and ask her what services are included if you were to buy the boots and blades from her. Then do the math.

* But even if there is a cost savings via the online route, remember the benefits of establishing a good relationship with a local competent seller; particularly, if you intend to stick with this sport for a while. [Though unfortunately the range of "local" will vary a lot depending on where you are.]

* Above all, ask around for who's good at specific tasks. E.g., one shop near me is owned and run by a guy who was an ex-competitive and ex-pro figure skater; now a coach. He knows figure skating, is knowledgeable about boots and blades, and is a good fitter. But his sharpening is mediocre.

* Right now you are in Artistes, a relatively soft recreational boot that is not closely form-fitted; it's also a bit too large; and you've just been starting out. So everything probably feels comfy and you don't feel you have any foot issues that need special attention. If you move to a more advanced boot however, all that will change. It will be stiffer and more closely form-fitted. Any mismatches between your feet and boots will become more apparent. And as you advance in your skills, working on deeper edges, spins, and jumps, proper positioning and alignment of the blades become more important.
