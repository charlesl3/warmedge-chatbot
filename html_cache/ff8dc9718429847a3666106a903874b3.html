Hi:
The quick and very general reply is that new skates are supposed to be stiff, to save you from ankle injuries (and others). Except for specific moves, the deeper your knees are bent, the better you'll skate, the more confident you'll feel, and the less often you'll fall. The more you skate, the boots will gradually break in by themselves.

Do your skates fit well everywhere else? (They're supposed to be snugger than your street shoes.) Did you buy them at a skate shop with the help of a fitter? Or was it the coach of your group class who recommended Jacksons for you? Is it around the top edge of the boot that you feel uncomfortable, or lower inside around your ankle bones? A bit more description and we can analyze a problem, if there is one and it's not just the unaccustomed feel of all new boots. Good luck!

I'm still a bit confused as to what you mean, since an extended free leg has to have a bent skating leg for basic stroking. It would only be straight if you're doing a spiral, or any of a lot of moves that are beyond your technical level at this stage. If you mean you can't stand up on or off the ice with your legs straight because the boot edge digs in (and I'd expect that then to be cutting in at the shin not the calf across the back), then that probably means you've been lacing them tightly right to the top. The fitter should have told you not to do that with new boots. At first, you leave the top hook or even two hooks unused and stop lacing at the next hook down, tying the laces off there. Then as you settle into the boots over the next little while -- impossible to say how long without knowing how many hours per week you skate -- you can gradually try extending the lacing to the top or almost. Personally, I never use the top hook. I always tie the laces off at the second hook down. But that's probably just a habit left over from my beginner days when boots were came higher up the shin. I need to land jumps and pairs throws with a deep knee bend, and I need that open feeling at the top of the laces to accommodate the bent leg.

Did the skate shop heat mold your boots for you when you bought them? They may need more molding in the ankle area. But I may have interpreted your description incorrectly, so please do explain more if you need to.

Do you mean that the tongue or the front of the boot gets in the way of straightening the leg? Or is the interaction in the back? Or at the level where your ankle bends? Could you be a bit more specific?

If you bought the boots at a store, go back to the store with your boots as soon as possible to see if they can help. In fact that should be your first step, before attempting any fixes. They will probably give guidance for free. If not, you could try taking your boots to a store to see if they have other suggestions. If you have a coach, they could possibly help too.

What tsop4me implied, and Diana expanded on, might help. I.E., maybe you want to not tie the laces all the way to the top hooks, at least at first. Or let them be a little loose at the level where the boot needs to bend when your ankle bends (perhaps skip that level of holes or hooks altogether).

If I tie my boots too tight, they interfere a bit. But only if I tie them very, very tight, especially near the ankle bend. But boot fit is a pretty complicated thing, so that may not apply to you.

On heat molding, Jackson's own pages are a bit ambiguous. Some 3rd party pages claim the Artiste is heat moldable. Other 3rd party pages claim the opposite. So I would strongly suggest you contact Jackson before attempting it, or have your pro shop attempt it. If they say no, heat molding is likely to damage the boot. If they say yes, you should ask for the molding temperature for a pro shop to use (they have special ovens that are better controlled than kitchen ovens).

Perhaps your boots are a bit too small for you, especially near the tops of the boots, because your leg is a bit wider than they designed for. You could try wearing very thin socks. Maybe removing the insoles and replacing them with something thinner - though that probably won't help, because the insoles probably aren't at the level that interferes - but doing so might lower your leg a little, so it is in a slightly larger portion of the boot. Since this is unlikely to help with this specific problem, try other things first.

* How do you come to this conclusion?

* As you mentioned in a previous thread, there is a jacksonultima and a jacksonskate website. I will refer to the jacksonultima website.

* Jackson groups their kits according to "Series". Boots within the same series use the same materials and construction, but have different values of stiffness. The Artiste is in the "Ascend Series". But to understand which models are heat moldable, we first need to delve into the more advanced "Fusion Series".

* First look at the "Fusion Series". The current Fusion Series comprises 3 models: (high to low) Freestyle, Elle, EVO.

* There is a tab "Heat Moldable Technology". Click on that. For the Fusion Series, they state:

"Our flagship technology, we design boots to fit tight when cold. With Jackson's Heat Moldable Fit System, your Skate Technician uses heat to soften and mold the boots. As the boot cools, it retains the shape of your foot.

Custom fit in minutes!"

* Click on Video 1: "Jackson Ultima Instant Custom Heat Molding". Starting at ~0:18, the tech states that starting with the Elle model, Jackson boots can achieve an instant custom fit via heat molding.

* Now click on Video 2: "Jackson Ultima Elle Fusion". Starting at ~0:24, the tech states that the Elle is the first skate in the lineup that is fully heat moldable.

* Now return to "Fusion Series". Click on "EVO 2020". Click on Video 3: "Jackson EVO Skate". Starting at ~0:24, the tech states that the EVO is fully heat moldable.

* So: (a) the Fusion Series is the first kit series that is heat moldable; and (b) there is inconsistency whether the Elle or the EVO is the lowest model to be heat moldable. [Additional info outside of what is posted is needed to resolve this. The videos were also posted on YouTube.: Video 1, 6 yrs ago; Video 2, 5 yrs ago; Video 3, 4 yrs ago. As stated in Video 3, the EVO was the latest addition to the Fusion Series. So at one time, the Elle was the lowest model, but then the EVO (lower than the Elle) was added. Video 1 and Video 2 need to be updated.]

* With that lengthy background, turn to the "Ascend Series", to which the Artiste belongs. The Ascend Series is lower than the Fusion Series. Note that there is no tab "Heat Moldable Technology". The only tab is "Perfect Match", some PR nonsense. The video "Jackson Ultima Ascend Series Figure Skates" has more PR nonsense. In particular, it does not state that the boots in the Ascend Series are heat moldable. If you return to the Ascend Series and click on the Artiste model for more info, there is also no statement that it is heat moldable (and no accompanying video either).

* So, based on the info Jackson provides for the Fusion Series and for the Ascend Series, how do you conclude that the info provided by Jackson on the heat moldability of the Artiste is "a bit ambiguous"?

There are a couple aspects of figure skates that initially felt awkward for me.

1. Like most people (according to some medical sources), my normal walking and running gaits includes bending my ankle outwards (to the side) when my heel touches the ground while that foot is in back, and back inwards, as my big toe pushes off while that foot is in front. That alternate "supination" and "pronation" helps drive the body forwards, though some barefoot walking enthusiasts claim it is a consequence of walking with shoes that have (somewhat) elevated and cushioned heels. (A PT told me that there is a tremendous variety of walking and running gaits, and he though most people should not alter the gait that feels natural.) But all reasonably decent figure skates, even fairly low level skates, are deliberately designed to reduce sideways ankle bend, to reduce the likelihood of ankle sprains and strains. That could make someone feel like the couldn't walk right. I'm not sure if it could make someone feel like they couldn't straighten their leg.

I noticed that first in downhill ski boots - especially downhill ski boots. The first time I put on downhill ski boots, I almost felt like I couldn't walk at all, only sort of waddle. I had to transition to using my hips to do some of what my ankles had been doing. I still find walking in downhill ski boots a little awkward.

By the time I started skating, I had already been skiing for a fair number of years. So this aspect of figure skates was not all that big a problem for me. But perhaps for a person new to figure skating, or who had only skated in used or rental skates that were too broken down to provide much ankle support, it could be a problem? I'm not sure.

2. Almost all figure skates have a heel that is much higher than a normal walking shoe. I'm told that for people accustomed to using lady's high heel fashion shoes this is not a problem - that many fashion shoes have much higher heels. (In fact medical sources say some ladies have the opposite problem - they may injure their Achilles tendon(s) by over-stretching them when walking or running using neutral heel shoes, or walking barefoot, because the Achilles tendons gradually become too short if they spend a lot of time in high heel shoes.)

But for me - and I assume other people who do not have much toe point flexibility, the high heel means I have a substantial amount of trouble moving. I can not straighten a leg easily when that foot is on the ice, even in those one foot high positions (such as at the top of a scratch spin, or a number of ice dance positions) where I was told to straighten it. And my upper body is always pushed forwards. Which means, for example, that I can't really throw my arms or legs into forwards jumps, because I would fall forwards. And that is after over 20 years skating. Which is why I personally am taking the ridiculously extreme step of looking to to cut down heels on skate boots that were only designed for high heels. (I'm not suggesting the o.p. do that. There are multiple obvious problems that could create, and probably a few that are not obvious to me.)

If heel height is the problem that the original poster feels, no amount of breaking in the boots, or lacing differently, is going to solve it. Maybe their ankles and connecting muscles and tendons will eventually stretch out enough to eliminate the feeling, and maybe they won't.

My best guess is that most people don't have this problem, and have better toe point flexibility than I do. If it were a problem for many people, I assume boot fitters would be told to test for it, and neutral heel shoes would be more common in shoe stores. So I have no special reason to believe it is the o.p.'s problem. I don't think that if it feels like the top of the boot is digging into the front or back of the foot, it is to likely to be the issue. Instead, it feels to me more like a balance issue, that I am almost constantly doing a low level fight against a forwards fall while in skates.

But if it is the problem, perhaps other types or brands of figure skating boots have lower heels. A skate tech claimed to me that Jackson boots have higher heels on average than some brands. But he didn't have enough brands in stock for me to figure out which brands have lower boots. (It doesn't help that some figure skate boots don't have the same internal and external heel elevation, so I can't tell from pictures.) The Riedell boots he had, had about the same heel height as same skating level Jacksons. A Jackson's phone rep told me that on full custom boots, they can, if requested, cut as much as 3/16" from heel height. (Perhaps not enough for me or some other people.)

Of course the o.p. has already purchased these boots, and may not wish to try other boots.

To the o.p.: forget all this discussion from people who haven't seen you try to use the skates, and just ask the shop, and/or a coach, or another experienced skater to help you figure out what is going on.

Maybe the boot fit is wrong for you. Maybe you simply aren't used to using the bent knee positions that high heels are to some extent meant to push you into. Maybe you aren't strong enough yet to hold those positions. Maybe the heels are too high for you. Etc.

Without seeing you, no one here can figure that out.


tstop4me is obviously right that the JacksonSkate.com doesn't include their upper level skates. So I don't get why the site exists at all, and why the boot prices are missing on JacksonUltima.com. And JacksonUltima.com is certainly organized in a very complicated manner. But perhaps they want people to mostly go through dealers to get info about boots, rather than figure everything out themselves from the websites - and that is what the Jackson customer rep really meant. Plus, she new that I was personally looking at the Jackson Freestyle model boot - which was described & priced on JacksonSkate.com.

"Heat Moldable Fit System" is prominently featured at the start of this page:
https://jacksonultima.com/pages/skates
The boots in the charts on that page includes the Artiste.
A reasonable person might assume that all of the boots in those charts are heat moldable. If that isn't true, Jackson could re-design the page to make the distinction clear.

Likewise,
https://jacksonultima.com/pages/jackson-fit-guide
has "Heat Moldable Fit System" at the end. That page does ask "Are your boots heat moldable?" But the page then kind of makes it look like all Jackson skates & boots are, because it doesn't say that any of them aren't. Or at least that is the most obvious reading of that page. Sure, if you click on other pages, you only find heat moldability mentioned on some of them. But a lot of people won't do that. Especially since the page twice suggests that you consult with an authorized Jackson Dealer and their skate technicians - which I assume many skaters then go on to do, rather than looking carefully at additional pages to see what is missing from them.

These are perfect examples of how web site designers often create web pages that are more beautiful than useful and informative.
