It is too late. The only internationals you are getting anywhere near are the Adult Internationals and those don't require the sort of intensity that those elite skaters do. (And you have to be 30 for those.)

Rippon is 28, but he's been skating for eighteen years, and 10 is a late start. Weir started at 12 because he was stupidly talented - and we're talking he landed an Axel in his first week levels of talented.

I will be utterly blunt about this: dropping out of school in year 10 to focus on skating is a dreadfully stupid idea. Even Brendan Kerry finished his HSC by correspondence from the US. University isn't for everyone, but having your entrance score by finishing year 12 keeps your options open. Year 10 gets you nowhere, trust me, unless you do a trade apprenticeship. Without Year 12 or a trade apprenticeship you are perilously close to condemning yourself to a lifetime at the minimum wage.

Nothing is more important than your education. And even if you did make a skating career - what comes after skating? Even the top GP and Olympic skaters know that skating doesn't last forever and unless you're in Russia or Japan you can't make your fortune from it so you never have to work. Max Aaron just retired at 26 - but he also studied and got his finance degree while training because he knew skating wouldn't be forever.

None of this means you can't start. None of this means you can't skate. But you have to be realistic. Every skater at the international level has been working their butts off for years by your age. An international career isn't in the cards for you. That's the cold, hard truth.

It usually takes 8-10 years to make a Senior International (at least). By then you'd be 23 at least which is well past your athletic peak and ancient for a ladies' skater.

And you can "afford everything you need"? Oh please. No, you can't, I guarantee that, not unless you have a spare $50,000+ a year sitting around. And don't get any crazy ideas about sponsorships in your head - our top skaters barely get any funding whatsoever, and no real sponsorships, and those are the Olympians, and you'll never be one of those.

I don't sugar-coat this. Probably other posters here will tell me I'm too harsh. But I'm tired of foolish teenagers who insult our sport by thinking it's so easy that they can just start late and make it to the top. You're old enough to learn how life works. And I react especially badly when I see terrible life decisions being made for a dream that will never come true.

I'm going to be short and to the point: don't do it.

Yes, you love skating now. First of all, you can't know if you'll love it in four years. What is more, you don't know if you'll be injured doing a waltz jump in a year, and if you leave your studies to be a waitress/cook/even restaurant owner, where does it leave you then? It wouldn't even be your own effort.
Second: living from skating is very very difficult. Even top competitors continue studying to have another field of work, and I'm talking of people who have actually won world championships. Both Adam and Johnny went to the media.
Third: longevity isn't the same as starting late. It's easier to keep your jumps if you had them as a child. Weir had an otherworldly talent. He had an axel in a week, do you?
Four: You're a girl. You've got way more competition, to absurd limits. The thing, it's not on you, it's that way more people have been training as hard as you say since way longer.

Now onto a bit of a happier message: Olympics is not the end all of the figure skating world. Nor is Gran Prix (and that screams "I just watched Yuri on ice!!! and I love it so I want to do the same", so let me tell you that while a good show it isn't 100% accurate). Getting into the senior circuit is super difficult per se (speaking about just Nationals), even more as a girl. The good news are: you don't need to throw your life out of the window to both enjoy skating and get something out of it: there's plenty of chances of competing at your age and see if you like it, and if you don't get to the senior circuit you could go to adult competitions, where chances are you'll be very good. I'm not saying "quit skating you won't go anywhere", I'm saying "don't quit anything due to skating, and be careful what you set your sights on. Be realistic".

Hi Jas! I respect you for having this dream and truly loving skating. If you want to do competitive skating, that's of course up to you. Just make sure you have your facts straight (cost, competition, etc) and go into it with your eyes wide open.

One thing I would like to say: Please DO NOT DROP OUT OF SCHOOL. Whether you become a competitive and eventually professional skater or not, you should keep your options open. Notice that most of the elite skaters are actually in school. All the Japanese skaters finished or are finishing high school and continued or are continuing on to university. Tatsuki Machida even went on to finish his masters and take up a PhD. Charlie White went on to study political science. Nathan Chen is going to Yale to take up biochemical engineering. Michelle Kwan finished international studies and political science from the University of Denver. Do not underestimate the power of education. Even the best skaters can only skate for so long. It will never be a stable lifelong career, so don't compromise your future by dropping out of school and destroying most of your chances to have a well-paying job. As you said, your mother worked hard to eventually earn well, so don't waste the opportunity she's giving you. She worked hard so you could have a good future.

You might think you learn nothing from school now, but even if you may not remember your lessons, they're there and will help you in many ways that you wouldn't recognize. Much like the food you ate a month ago, you may not remember the meal but it still nourished you. Finish all your grade levels, then you can choose a specific course in university that will be of interest to you. If you drop out of school now, the likelihood of you getting to a good university and a good education is almost nothing.

Hence, skate if you want to, but don't drop out of school.

I'm in the US.

I once knew a girl who started skating at 14 because she wanted to be a skating coach. I watched her progress through her teens -- she had reached a middle level of proficiency (including double jumps, but not great ones) by the time I went elsewhere.

Making a career teaching low-level skaters was probably a reasonable goal for her, if she continued to be interested in learning how to teach as well as how to skate. But it's not a glamorous or high-paying lifestyle.

Within the US testing structure, "Open Juvenile" was the level she would best be able to compete at, and then later in adult competitions if she liked. Senior-level competition, where triple jumps are required and the strong skaters do all the triples easily and well and in combination, and move across the ice with an ease developed from early childhood, was not in the cards for her and she understood that. She may have eventually passed senior tests, but the skill level needed to pass the US tests is far less than the level needed to compete at the respective competition levels.

You might be able to achieve similar goals in an Australian context. I don't know if you're interested in teaching skating, but if the skating population in Australia is much smaller than in the US, then the career prospects would be correspondingly slimmer, competing for jobs against skaters with higher level experience.

Many skating coaches also need other sources of income in the US and undoubtedly that is true in Australia as well. Also many good coaches study relevant fields at university that help them understand the teaching process in general and for physical skills specifically.

Like previous posters, I'd recommend that you skate as much as you like and can afford (in terms of both time and money), develop your skills at the rate you are able, and continue to adjust your short-term and long-term goals as you see what kind of progress you make. Don't make life decisions that will cut off your prospects for supporting yourself as an adult either outside skating or within the sport at levels you can achieve.

For most skaters, even high-level competitors, skating is a hobby that costs money, not a way of earning money. If you're realistic about that fact, you can enjoy the training and whatever you're able to do with it in realistic terms.

There seems to be no content in the original post, so I am going only on information extracted from previous responses.

My first question is, have you told your mother about your ideas and gotten her feedback and approval for what you want to do with skating, education, and your future as it is impacted by these things? At your age, if your mom doesn't agree to follow your plan with her money and support (which will be needed way into adulthood I you drop out of school), then there is no point to further discussion.

I am also wondering if your ideas about limiting your education are based somewhat on the possibility that you will always have a job in a family owned business? If you plan on taking over when your mother wants to retire, you will need some kind of business and restaurant management education, and unless it is a franchise, having education in nutritionist and culinary trends will also be helpful. Employees don't always have real respect for bosses who do not have more education than they do, and might just see you as an under-educated person who got lucky enough to inherit a business but doesn't have enough education to keep it running at full speed. The restaurant business can be booming one year and busted the next, for a wide variety of reasons, so the education is crucial.

What exactly are you seeing yourself doing with skating once you are past the stage of competition? Being a coach usually means you have to pay for things that eat away the income, and the income is not always steady since it is completely dependent on the skaters. Almost all the coaches I know, have some kind of college degree, usually in a field related to education, sports, health, or psychology. Some have to work at multiple rinks to make enough money, some do other jobs. None of them makes enough money to live a very glamourous life, even the top level ones. It might be useful to ask coaches about how they are able to live on just coaching, if that is all they are doing.

It is very easy to fall in love with a special interest as a teenager, and want to engulf oneself in it, planning an entire life around it. But the majority of people who have those dreams and aspirations as teens, find themselves making adjustments along the way, as they mature and discover the realities of life. If you are ever planning to have a family, that is going to need to be figured into your plans, because you probably won't be able to support one on an ice skating career, especially if you have children. Just keep a level head and don't make any rush decisions.

So let's say you are truly gifted and also have enough money to train for 20 hours a week in Australia for the next several years, including 10 hours per week of private lessons with Australia's best coach(es). Then you reach the point where you need to do what the top Australian skaters do. So then you move to Los Angeles, where you will need to pay for your own apartment ($2,000/month, maybe less if you can find a roommate), car and auto insurance ($300/mo ifyou get a cheap, used car), ice time and lessons. 20 hours a week of freestyle ice at $13/hr, and 10 hours of lesson time per week $110 per hour ( I know Frank Carroll costs more). You will also need some sort off-ice training for strengthening and injury prevention. That would be at least $50 per week, even if you just have the gym membership and a few introductory training sessions with a trainer to get you started. Each pair of boots and blades will cost $1,300, and you'll go through 2 pairs per year doing doubles and 3-4 pairs per year doing triples, and definitely 4 doing quads. Since the U.S. requires that you have medical insurance but you have to pay for it yourself, you'll need to add that as well. Since you're young, it might only be around $500 per month.

If you assume you take 2 weeks off per year, you will need more than $70,000 per year for your training itself, $3,900 for 3 pairs of skates per year (being optimistic), $3600 for a car, $24,000 for rent, $6,000 for medical insurance, and $7,300 for food if you only spend $20 per day. That's a budget of $115,300 per year if you spend $0 on clothes, entertainment, actually going to a doctor (which is only partially covered by insurance here), telephone, competition costumes, entry fees and travel, or flying home to visit your parents.

So I think the first thing you need to do is to find out if you are talented enough to make this happen. Get the right boots that fit you perfectly, make sure your blades are also good, then give yourself a trial period of three months doing 20 hours a week of on-ice and off-ice training, including 10 hours of private lesson time each week with a good coach. If you are landing clean doubles and completing clean combination spins in all 3 positions with difficult variations and on both feet after that period of time, then your progress is at the right pace and you may be talented enough to keep going and see where it takes you. None of this is to discourage you, just to help you plan and know what to expect. Doubles take 3 times as long to get as singles, triples take 3 times as long as doubles, etc. etc., even if you are very gifted.

Just my 2cents here as a parent of a high level skater -yes, my kid is one of those who started at a young and competing at the national level.
No we are not $$$ -we work hard and sacrifice a lot because of the passion and dreams... if the dream becomes reality that is bonus.
Reality is being able to work and pay your way through post-secondary school with a job in what you love and possibly even make a living coaching.

Reality is that most kids (not including those super talented/gifted skaters) competing at a national level usually have their single axel by the time they are 8 or so, all doubles within 2 years of getting their axel and triples 2-3 years afterwards. Getting the jumps is very different vs having them consistent vs being able to compete with them consistently.
The current thoughts from coaches/experts are that triples are easier to maintain and work through puberty than trying to learn them afterwards -not to say it isn't possible -it's just a lot harder.

Do not quit school. School actually helps balance out your activities regardless of your gifts /if you have a plan in place or not for your future education. It helps you focus and have better time management in general. It keeps you in touch with your peers and gives you a social outlet outside of skating.

If you have your double axel and your triples including one or 2 triple-triples -which is when most teen skaters need to start looking at what their future holds including going down to a part time school or in Olympic year, put the 1st semester on hold and focus only on skating. You/your parent can work with the school and arrange a good schedule if the school is willing to accommodate you based on your achievements.

If you get injured, you need school to be there to get your mind off of things and keep occupied while you are healing.

$ can get you the material things needed but $ does not buy talent, $ does not buy good work ethics. Talent does not equate good work ethics necessarily either.

I am not trying to be harsh here but I've seen very very talented kids incl Jr World Champions go down the drain and not make it at the Senior Int'l level which is extremely sad to see because of $, work ethics or just plan bad luck on injuries.

It is great to see you want to do this full time but as many other posters have pointed out, you still have a long road ahead of you and there is no need to make drastic decisions now -take it year by year and re-evaluate year by year. If you do get to a point where, you say 'hey... maybe I am within reach of my dream' that's really a bonus.

Don't forget.. skating stays with you for life. There are so many avenues of competition available including adult competition or just plain skating to enjoy it.

Good luck.
