I didn't see the OP, but man, some of the replies are quite harsh. There is a way to coax somebody out of bad life decisions and an open hand sometimes works better than a closed fist. Honesty can still have empathy.

I actually don't think 10 years old is impossibly late to become an elite skater (as in competing at the National level - not as in going to the Olympics which tons of amazing, lifelong-trained skaters will never do). The chances of that are slim at this point though. The amount of dedication you need, and of course, the financial burden, is magnified that much more because you haven't spent your early years learning basics and fundamentals of jumping and spinning, or even just skating skills; the muscle memory isn't developed. You're starting a 100 meter race, 20 metres back from the start line.

As mentioned, DEFINITELY do not quit school - for figure skating or any athletic endeavour. All it takes is one career-ending injury and life will hit you hard. And especially when you become an adult, you won't necessarily be able to depend on the support that you are getting from your family now.

I think it was a great suggestion to try a 3-month trial period where you intensely train (and focus the money on training - working out, learning basics and building toughness and endurance -- not things like fancy dresses or travelling to competitions) and see if you can progress to solid spins and double jumps. Since I can't see the OP, it's hard to say what type of an athlete you are (if you have natural endurance and flexibility, for example). Then see if you've got what it takes to compete (while still training intensely). It's one thing to have a dream, but you have to work extra hard even on days you don't want to - and there will be many of those. You also have to be absolutely open to harsh and intense criticism, if you want to get to a level that is competitive and be ready to accept criticism as well as respond to it and measure up to it. This is a subjective sport and people can be brutal (as you've seen), but that's how people get to the elite level.

One more thing: don't lose sight of enjoying skating... if your only goal is to get to a certain level or compete at a certain competition, your self-worth and self-esteem will hinge on that, and skating will become something you inevitably end up hating. Train hard, but also be happy.... and be ready to switch gears to something else if it doesn't work out (which is a veritable possibility you need to be prepared for mentally, physically, and financially).

I'll share my own experience. I didn't start skating until I was around 10 or 11 and I was often the oldest kid in the group class. I kept skating until middle school and went to a bunch of competitions but I started to become very self conscious about how much older I was than all the 5-8 year olds and I hadn't made much progress (only had a waltz jump and a half flip). So I quit and didn't pick it up again until my late 20s. Now I'm having so much fun with it and I do adult competitions. In the past 3 years I've learned nearly all of my single jumps except axel and can do back spins which eluded me as a kid. My point is that you have to just skate for you and know your own limits. Sometimes I wish I had kept skating when I was younger but looking back now I had the mindset that I wanted to be an elite skater and once I realized it would never happen I just quit. It's important to be realistic and to figure out why you want to skate. If it's only to be an elite skater you might want to reconsider or else change your mindset.

If OP is set on competing in ladies singles, she would need one triple to pass her senior test and make it to nationals, if she stuck it out long enough. But it's impossible to get sent internationally without the minimum technical scores, and if she's 14/15 now with only single jumps, we already have a whole slew of kids in that age bracket who have their double jumps.

We usually only have one or two teams competing in senior pairs/dance, so if you can pass all the tests and go to nationals, you can be a national medallist. Again, it doesn't mean anything in an international context if the minimum technical scores are not achieved.

So yes, getting to the nationals here isn't quite as competitive as it is in other countries, but you'd still have to be skating at a particular level to pass the tests to qualify, and getting to that level with such a late start is pretty much impossible.
