At a fairly beginner level still, you already have two lessons a week. You don't need a third coaching session a week, you need to use that time for practice ice. I've heard that for every 30 minute lesson, you need at least 2 hours of dedicated practice time to see that improvement.

I'm not a jumper, so I never got an axel before switching to ice dance (yay knee problems). I take two lessons a week: one with a dance coach, one with my main coach. I am on the ice at least 4-6 hours a week outside of that for practice, when my client deadlines aren't insane (I'm a freelancer). Rather than focusing on when you're going to get your axel, focus on making your current elements the strongest they can be because that will make the axel easier when it comes along. If your edges aren't great, or you can't hold your backspin consistently, you're going to run into a wall with the axel until those are solid.

As others have said, this is dependent on:
- your natural ability
- the amount of time you practice, and practice well
- your coach (experience, style, connections are all important here - a coach who can collaborate with other coaches to help their students is probably going to be a better coach than one who thinks they know everything)

other factors like:
- if your rink has a harness and if your coach uses it because that can offer the ability to practice with less injury, which can reduce fear and help you learn more quickly
- how hard you fall and your willingness to try again
- your pads and equipment (you're not going far with bad skates, for instance)

Here's what I learned from other threads here and from reading coaching materials and resources for parents:

There are bottlenecks at basically every axel. A significant number of people drop out at single axel, and then again at double. For boys/men, triple is another one. People struggle with the axels.
So basically, it's not a given that you will learn it. Many don't.
I'm not saying that to be discouraging, only to explain that it's hard.
The kids I know who have their axel, there are some who learned it on the first official lesson and then there are others who took a year to learn it. They practice the same amount of time, they have the same coach, similar ages etc. But some kids just 'get it' easier than others.

Men typically can start skating in singles and definitely in pairs and dance later because they don't go through puberty changes in the same way that women do. Skating from an early age delays the onset of puberty in women, and the severity of the body changes in puberty when it does begin is often reduced, when training at an elite-track level - although it still requires an adjustment period. So girls who master their doubles and are set on triples before puberty have a higher probability of landing them than girls who start later. If you start at 13 or 14 you're likely already developing hips and breasts and your body has gone through irreversible changes. It makes it harder to jump for the majority of women. Meanwhile, during puberty guys develop muscular and bone stature that HELPS them, if they are already the ideal body type for skating. They can start later and skate longer at the elite level... It's the same in gymnastics. They say women need to get to a certain level before puberty to make it in elite gymnastics, and men can start later and compete later as well. It's really unfair, but it's that much harder for women to compete in their twenties and thirties, although I am happy that we are seeing skaters like Carolina Kostner and Ashley Wagner continue to compete. But they started at a young age, and they aren't doing triple-triple combinations at the same rate as the young girls, even if they used to land them. Girls have a better shot in dance than in singles and pairs if they start later, but they have to find a partner, and guys are scarce.

There may also be a fear factor that comes into play at puberty for women, whereas men don't have that same instinct.
