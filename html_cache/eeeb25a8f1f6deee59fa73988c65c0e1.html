I think the problem is that, as someone else pointed out, OP is young (well, a teenager). I thought I was smart as a teenager but I had zero real life experience which affected my outlook on life. I note that OP justifies pursuing her dream of skating competitively by pointing out that her family turned their financial situation around with hard work and hope and luck.

Unfortunately as figure skating is very much a sport dominated by (for females at any rate) pre-pubescent kids and teens, it's not really comparable. There's no real time limit on creating a successful business and improving your financial situation - you can do that at any age, any time in life. Highly competitive sports, especially ones like figure skating and gymnastics, do have a very real time/age limit to get good. It sucks but it's the reality. I also question OP's (and her family's) dedication to the sport if she's had ill-fitting skates since Christmas - which are apparently holding her back - but still doesn't have new ones (and created a GoFundMe to fund them??). This doesn't seem like a situation worth quitting school over.

I didn't see the original post as it's been deleted apparently so I don't know what the question was but OP, sorry you didn't get the encouragement you were after. You've been given some fantastic advice though. Unfortunately the real world is frequently disappointing.

Also at 14 you will probably change your mind every 6-12 months about what you want to do in the future. Don't shut off things like uni/tec needlessly. I'm not even saying you should go to uni or tec - just that you should leave that option open to you in case you change your mind and you're disappointed in 4 years. If you want to teach skating, that's awesome - but you don't need to quit school to do that. I've got a friend who learned violin to a very high level and then taught violin for many years - while also completing a university degree. If you're a hard worker, you can achieve many things. Completing high school education while becoming e.g. a skating instructor is a totally achievable goal.

Good luck with whatever you choose to do in life!

I missed this part. I think this is a bad sign. This is the kind of sport that requires a sugar daddy. It's not the kind of sport that you can do all out if you're getting equipment only for birthdays and Christmas. You have to be willing and able to fix problems as they arise so you need to be able to get new skates when needed even if you just got them, drive long distances to sharpen blades, pay hefty competition entry fees for competitions that are 6 weeks away, not knowing what your situation will be when it comes around, pay coaching fees just to have a coach stand next to you and put you on the ice.

When that competition comes around, you have to have a parent who can afford to take time off work because there's a chance you'll skate on a Thursday. You have to be able to afford hotels for competitions, dresses, etc. And if you don't compete at the lower levels, you won't really have much of an idea of how you compare to others, let alone the experience and poise to compete at a much larger competition when the groups are 15+ girls.

It doesn't wait for your next birthday or GoFundMe to come through. A serious skater can't just say, "well, these skates are all wrong but I guess I'll keep using them for 6 more months..."

It is too late. The only internationals you are getting anywhere near are the Adult Internationals and those don't require the sort of intensity that those elite skaters do. (And you have to be 30 for those.)

Rippon is 28, but he's been skating for eighteen years, and 10 is a late start. Weir started at 12 because he was stupidly talented - and we're talking he landed an Axel in his first week levels of talented.

I will be utterly blunt about this: dropping out of school in year 10 to focus on skating is a dreadfully stupid idea. Even Brendan Kerry finished his HSC by correspondence from the US. University isn't for everyone, but having your entrance score by finishing year 12 keeps your options open. Year 10 gets you nowhere, trust me, unless you do a trade apprenticeship. Without Year 12 or a trade apprenticeship you are perilously close to condemning yourself to a lifetime at the minimum wage.

Nothing is more important than your education. And even if you did make a skating career - what comes after skating? Even the top GP and Olympic skaters know that skating doesn't last forever and unless you're in Russia or Japan you can't make your fortune from it so you never have to work. Max Aaron just retired at 26 - but he also studied and got his finance degree while training because he knew skating wouldn't be forever.

None of this means you can't start. None of this means you can't skate. But you have to be realistic. Every skater at the international level has been working their butts off for years by your age. An international career isn't in the cards for you. That's the cold, hard truth.

It usually takes 8-10 years to make a Senior International (at least). By then you'd be 23 at least which is well past your athletic peak and ancient for a ladies' skater.

And you can "afford everything you need"? Oh please. No, you can't, I guarantee that, not unless you have a spare $50,000+ a year sitting around. And don't get any crazy ideas about sponsorships in your head - our top skaters barely get any funding whatsoever, and no real sponsorships, and those are the Olympians, and you'll never be one of those.

I don't sugar-coat this. Probably other posters here will tell me I'm too harsh. But I'm tired of foolish teenagers who insult our sport by thinking it's so easy that they can just start late and make it to the top. You're old enough to learn how life works. Yes, it's frustrating to see teenagers post this question, over and over. But let's remember that it's a NEW question to THEM. With that in mind, let's try to remember that teenagers don't realize how little they know about life yet. To a teenager, for example, $115,000 is a lot of money, and it's hard to imagine that that wouldn't be enough to pay for their training. It's also hard for a teenager new to the sport to grasp the amount of time and energy invested in order to master triple jumps and get them consistent in competition. It's something you can't conceive of if you have just learned all the single jumps and haven't spent years at the rink watching skaters crash and burn trying to master the double axel, only to end up quitting due to injury and/or the inability to progress past that point. So let's try to be patient and kind. Honest about what they're up against, but with a little empathy.
